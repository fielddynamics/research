# Twice Over

*The twofold structure of gravity across five formalisms · Gravity Field Dynamics (GFD) · Field Dynamics Research*

---

Look closely at almost any serious theory of gravity and something appears in pairs.

- **Spinor matter.** Matter that spins comes in two handednesses, left and right, and coupling it to gravity requires a frame, the tetrad.
- **Massive gravitons.** Give the graviton a mass and the theory develops a fatal instability, unless a second metric is added beside the first.
- **Loop quantum gravity.** The Lorentz algebra, the mathematics of rotations and boosts, splits into two mirror-image halves, and loop quantum gravity was built on one of them.
- **Spinors.** The group that acts on spinors covers the rotations twice: a spinor needs two full turns, 720°, to come back to itself.
- **Stability.** The stability analysis of massive gravity says the same thing in its own terms: two metrics, with a carefully chosen coupling between them, or the theory fails.

Each of these is a well-established result in its own field, stated in its own language. Gravity Field Dynamics asks whether they are five facts, or one.

## The GFD reading

In GFD, the local unit of the gravitational field is not one tetrahedral chamber but two of opposite orientation, TetA and TetB, interpenetrating to form the stellated octahedron and coupled at a single central point, the Field Origin.

The two chambers are not assumed. The equation that relates the tetrad to the metric is quadratic in the frame, so it has two roots, related by a mirror reflection, and the metric cannot tell them apart. Those two roots are TetA and TetB.

On this reading, the pairs that turn up across physics are five views of the same object:

| Formalism | Its twofold structure | In GFD |
|---|---|---|
| Tetrad gravity and spinor matter | two chiralities, coupled through the tetrad | the two oppositely oriented chambers, two roots of one tetrad equation |
| Bimetric gravity | two metrics, two interacting tetrads | the two chambers; the Hassan–Rosen coefficients fixed at $\beta = \{1, 0, 16, 0, 1\}$ by $K = 4$ |
| Loop quantum gravity | self-dual and anti-self-dual halves; the quantum tetrahedron | one operator, the Hodge star, both exchanges the chambers and splits the Lorentz algebra |
| Spinor theory | the double cover $SL(2,\mathbb C)$ | each chamber realises one sheet; one turn realigns TetA, two realign both |
| Stability | a ghost unless two metrics and a special potential | 7 bimetric modes × 4 faces = 28, the element count of the stellated octahedron |

## What the note does

The working note, [*The Dual-Chamber Structure Across Gravitational Formalisms*](dual-chamber-requirement-across-formalisms.md), takes each formalism in turn. It states what the formalism establishes, with its primary sources, from Cartan, Weyl and Fock through Ashtekar, Rovelli and Smolin, de Rham–Gabadadze–Tolley and Hassan–Rosen. It then says how GFD connects that result to the stellated octahedron.

Three further parts give it substance:

- **The geometry.** Split the eight corners of a cube by the parity of their three-bit addresses, and two regular tetrahedra fall out, disjoint and sharing a centre. The note follows this construction through the exterior algebra, where the Hodge star turns out to be both the chamber exchange and the source of gravitational chirality.
- **What GFD derives that the formalisms assume.** Bimetric gravity postulates its second metric, and the Ashtekar formulation chooses one chiral half. GFD derives the pair from a single tetrad: two bounded regions from one frame, two components of one solution space, a geometry forced by symmetry, and a torsion sector that carries the distinction.
- **Open questions and predictions.** The note sets out five questions the comparison raises, among them how GFD's torsion differs from Einstein–Cartan torsion, and how its two chambers relate to the fixed and dynamical second metrics of massive gravity. It closes with the predictions the dual-chamber reading makes that no single formalism makes on its own, from the radial acceleration relation to Tully–Fisher evolution.
