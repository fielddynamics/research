## Two discoveries, three hundred years apart

On 1 April 1703, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz opened a letter from Peking. Joachim Bouvet, a Jesuit mathematician at the Kangxi court, had written it seventeen months earlier, and it had travelled by way of England. With it was a woodcut of the sixty-four hexagrams of the *I Ching*, the *Book of Changes*, in the arrangement attributed to the sage Fu Xi. Leibniz had been working on binary arithmetic for more than twenty years. In the broken and unbroken lines he recognised his own numbers, 0 to 63, written out in a figure drawn long before he was born. Within a week he sent his *Explication de l'arithmétique binaire* to the Paris Academy, with the hexagrams as its witness.

In 1995, at Simon Fraser University in British Columbia, Peter Borwein and Simon Plouffe noticed that the binary digits of $\log 2$ could be computed one at a time, deep in the expansion, without the digits before them. Plouffe then went looking for a formula that would do the same for π. He used an integer-relation program written by David Bailey, an implementation of Helaman Ferguson's PSLQ algorithm. The search turned up something no one had expected:

$$\pi = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{16^{k}}\left[\frac{4}{8k+1} - \frac{2}{8k+4} - \frac{1}{8k+5} - \frac{1}{8k+6}\right].$$

This is the Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe formula, BBP for short, published in 1997. Its remarkable property is that it lets you compute the millionth hexadecimal digit of π without computing any of the 999,999 digits before it. (The digits there are 2, 6, C, 6, 5, E, 5, 2, …; the tests below go and fetch them.) A number that had been studied for four thousand years turned out to be readable, digit by digit, at any position.

The two discoveries look unrelated: one belongs to divination and philosophy, the other to computational number theory. This note explains why they describe the same object, the stellated octahedron that Gravity Field Dynamics places at the centre of the gravitational field. It also explains how much of that can now be proved.

## The book is a cube

A trigram is three stacked lines, each either yang (unbroken) or yin (broken). There are eight of them.

Read the three lines, bottom to top, as three coordinates, each $+1$ or $-1$. Each trigram becomes a corner of a cube. That much is binary arithmetic, and Leibniz saw it. What he did not have the geometry to see is that the *I Ching*'s own organisation of the trigrams draws a specific figure inside that cube.

**The families are two tetrahedra.** The *Shuo Gua*, the "Discussion of the Trigrams" that accompanies the book, arranges the eight as a family:
- the father is Qian ☰, Heaven, three yang lines, and the mother is Kun ☷, Earth, three yin;
- the six children each have one distinctive line: one yang line among two yin for a son, one yin line among two yang for a daughter.

Gather the father and his three sons, and you have four corners of the cube that are all the same distance from one another. They form a regular tetrahedron. The mother and her three daughters form the other. The family is the stellated octahedron: two interlocking tetrahedra that share a centre.

**The opposites are the body diagonals.** The traditional opposite pairs are Heaven and Earth, Mountain and Lake, Thunder and Wind, and Water and Fire. In each pair every line is reversed, so each pair sits at opposite corners of the cube. Each pair joins one family to the other, and all four pairs cross at the centre, the Field Origin. Heaven and Earth, the father and the mother, mark the axis that Gravity Field Dynamics calls TIME.

**The hexagrams are the whole state space.** A hexagram stacks two trigrams. That makes sixty-four of them, which is every ordered pair of corners: sixteen from each family to each family.

**The book also says which line is which.** All of this holds whichever line is assigned to which axis, so the geometry alone cannot settle that choice. The *Shuo Gua* settles it. It ranks the children by where their distinctive line falls. The text tells it as a story of seeking:

> 震一索而得男，故謂之長男。⋯ 坎再索而得男，故謂之中男。⋯ 艮三索而得男，故謂之少男。
>
> *Zhen: at the first seeking, a son, so it is called the eldest son. … Kan: at the second seeking, a son, so it is called the middle son. … Gen: at the third seeking, a son, so it is called the youngest son.* (Shuo Gua, section 10; the omitted clauses give the daughters.)

The daughters, Xun, Li and Dui, are obtained the same way, at the first, second and third seeking. Set the text beside the figures and the ordinal is exactly the position of the child's distinctive line, counted from the bottom: Zhen's yang line is at the bottom, Kan's in the middle, Gen's at the top, and likewise for the daughters' yin lines. Of the six possible assignments of lines to coordinates, exactly one makes the sons' labels run 4, 2, 1 in order of age. It is the same assignment that turns Shao Yong's eleventh-century numbering of the trigrams into binary counting. The book carries its own coordinate system.

None of this needs interpretation. It is a set of identities, checked line by line in the proof.

## π has many formulas, and the geometry picks one

BBP has a base of $16 = K^2$, a period of $8 = 2^D$, and four active terms. These are the numbers of the stellated octahedron, with $K = 4$ faces per tetrahedron and $D = 3$ dimensions. Its eight possible positions, $8k+1$ through $8k+8$, can be laid on the cube's eight corners: position $j$ goes to the corner labelled $j \bmod 8$.

That raises an obvious objection. Is BBP special, or is it just one way among many of writing π?

In fact it is one among many. In 1997 Victor Adamchik and Stan Wagon showed that π has a whole family of formulas of this shape, one for every number $r$:

$$w(r) \;=\; \text{BBP} \;+\; r \times (8,\,-8,\,-4,\,-8,\,-2,\,-2,\,1,\,0).$$

The extra term adds up to exactly zero, so every member of the family still equals π. Panel B shows four of them. At $r = -\tfrac14$, for instance, the family produces another compact formula, with base $-4$.

**The first new result is that this family is all of them.** Every formula for π with base 16, period 8 and simple denominators is one of the $w(r)$. The proof runs as follows:
1. Split each of the eight sums around the eighth roots of unity. This writes each one through just seven logarithms: of $-1$, $2$, $1+\sqrt2$, $2 \pm i$ and $\sqrt2 \pm i$.
2. Show that those seven logarithms cannot be traded for one another. The argument is the prime factorisation of numbers built from $\sqrt2$ and $i$, which keeps the primes above 2, 3 and 5 apart.
3. Invoke Alan Baker's theorem of 1966, which extends that independence from rational to algebraic coefficients.

What remains is a small linear system with one free direction, and that direction is the Adamchik–Wagon term.

**The second result is the selection.** Ask that the TIME axis stay silent: father and mother carry no weight. Here the family holds a small surprise. The mother is already silent in every formula for π of this kind; not one of them uses her corner. The father is silent in exactly one. So exactly one formula for π leaves the TIME axis empty, and it is BBP.

The condition is not an easy one to meet. Try silencing each of the other three body diagonals instead. Thunder–Wind and Water–Fire leave no formula at all. Mountain–Lake leaves one, at $r = -\tfrac12$, but it puts three of its corners in one family and one in the other.

Nothing else was asked of it, yet the rest follows:
- It uses exactly four corners, Mountain, Thunder, Fire and Lake.
- Two of them come from each family, which is the balance between the two chambers.
- Its weights, $+4 - 2 - 1 - 1$, sum to zero, which is closure.

The figure shows this at a glance. Each row lights some corners and leaves others dark. Kun's column stays dark in every row, and only one row leaves Qian's dark as well.

**Why the answer is π.** A careful reader will ask whether this is special to π. Silence on the TIME axis turns out to pick out one formula for *any* constant that has one. That part is a property of the formula space, not of π. The question is what the chosen formula looks like.

Every formula of this kind, for any constant, comes out as a rational combination of just seven numbers: π, $\ln 2$, $\ln 3$, $\ln 5$, $\arctan\tfrac12$, $\sqrt2\ln(1+\sqrt2)$ and $\sqrt2\arctan(1/\sqrt2)$. Ask each of the seven for a formula that leaves the parents silent and also closes, its weights summing to zero:
- $\ln 2$ cannot even keep the parents silent, because it lives only in the mother's corner;
- the five others manage silence but never closure;
- only π does both.

Try every arrangement of BBP's weights over the octahedron's balanced corners, 216 in all, and the only one that gives a single constant is BBP itself. One more check: in base 16, the shortest period that can write π at all is 8, one slot for each corner of the cube.

## Where π actually comes from

If BBP is the octahedron's formula, where in it does the circle constant come from? Written as a single integral, BBP is

$$\pi = 16\int_0^1 \frac{y-1}{(y^2-2)(y^2-2y+2)}\,dy .$$

The denominator has two factors.
- **The factor that produces π.** $y^2 - 2y + 2$ has roots $1 \pm i$, which point at forty-five degrees. Its arctangent supplies π itself, as $4 \arctan 1$.
- **The factor that tidies up.** $y^2 - 2$ has roots $\pm\sqrt2$. It contributes nothing to π. Its job is to cancel a logarithm the first factor leaves behind.

Seen from the full integrand, the BBP weights do something tidy. The eight poles of $16 - y^8$ sit on a regular octagon, which is two squares. The BBP numerator cancels exactly half of them, keeping the real pair $\pm\sqrt2$ and the pair $1 \pm i$.

Both numbers live in the stellated octahedron. Every edge of a tetrahedron is a diagonal of a face of the cube, so the ratio of tetrahedron edge to cube edge is $\sqrt2$. Each of those edges crosses its face at forty-five degrees.

A single square has those two numbers as well. So this account shows how π enters, but on its own it does not require the full octahedron. That needs the selection by the TIME axis, the step that involves both tetrahedra.

## The family, read in order, writes the formula

There is a gentler way to see BBP inside the *I Ching*, and it is the one the earlier research notes found first.

List the father's tetrahedron in the order the *Shuo Gua* gives: Qian, then the eldest, middle and youngest sons. From each trigram's lines take two things:
- a **weight**: twice the bottom line, plus the middle line, plus the top line;
- a **step**: three places forward if the top line is yang, one if it is yin.

| | Lines (bottom, middle, top) | Position | Step | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qian, father | yang, yang, yang | 1 | 3 | +4 |
| Zhen, eldest son | yang, yin, yin | 4 | 1 | −2 |
| Kan, middle son | yin, yang, yin | 5 | 1 | −1 |
| Gen, youngest son | yin, yin, yang | 6 | 3 | −1 |

Out comes BBP: positions 1, 4, 5, 6 with weights $+4, -2, -1, -1$. The four steps add up to eight, one full turn around the cube. Read the mother and her daughters the same way, with every line reversed, and they produce the same formula.

The order matters. The three sons can be listed in six ways, and those six orderings are exactly the six symmetries of the octahedron that leave the TIME axis in place. All six give weights summing to zero, but only the order of age gives π. The others give nearby numbers such as $\ln(675/256) + 2\arctan 2 \approx 3.18$.

To be clear about what is shown here: the two reading rules (the weight and the step) were found by fitting to BBP. They are compact, and among 625 simple weight rules of the same linear form, only this one gives π; a test checks every one of them. They are not yet derived from the geometry or from the text, and the main result does not depend on them. What this section shows is that, written in the trigram's own lines, BBP comes out in the *I Ching*'s order of the children and in no other.

## A ladder through π

BBP's original surprise was that it reaches into π at any depth. To find the digit at position $n$, you work with the formula's four terms at position $n$ only, and you never need the digits before it. You can step onto any rung of the ladder directly.

Because $8 = 2^3$ and $16 = 2^4$, base 8 and base 16 are the same binary stream cut into groups of three and four bits. The two groupings line up again every twelve bits: three hexadecimal digits make four octal ones. So the ladder reaches the base-8 digits of π as well, and a base-8 digit is a trigram.

- **The ladder holds.** At 300 positions chosen at random out to the ten-thousandth digit, every trigram computed from the ladder alone matches the digits computed independently. Sent to the millionth hexadecimal place, the ladder returns 26C65E52, the digits David Bailey published there.
- **The walk can run on BBP alone.** Gravity Field Dynamics' closure walk starts at π's leading 3 and adds each base-8 digit, moving around the cube. Run on ladder digits, it is the same walk, and it closes all sixty-four hexagrams at step 332.
- **The ladder runs far.** Trigrams near the 100,000th place, each from its own rung, match a full arbitrary-precision computation. Over 2,000 consecutive windows, three ladder hexadecimal digits spell exactly four trigrams.
- **The ladder is the family's, not BBP's alone.** Every formula for π of this kind can extract digits the same way. BBP's advantage is economy: four terms instead of six or seven.
- **Base 8 belongs to the ladder.** For bases that are not powers of two, base 10 included, π has no ladder of this kind (Borwein, Borwein and Galway, 2004). Base 8 is the one ladder base whose digits are trigrams.

## What this does not say

Some things the work does not claim, and some it rules out.

- **No claim about intent.** Nothing here suggests the authors of the *I Ching* knew about π or about tetrahedra. The claim is structural: the book's organisation of the trigrams is the stellated octahedron, and the octahedron's TIME axis selects BBP from π's formulas.
- **The walk's statistics are ordinary.** Driven by π, the closure walk behaves statistically like a walk driven by any well-mixed digits. It closes at a typical step, and it does not visit the corners in the *Shuo Gua*'s order. π is not random: it is fixed by a four-term formula and can be read at any position, which no random sequence can. But its digit frequencies look random. The case for π rests on the formula, not on the walk's statistics.
- **One choice remains.** Reading the term $8k+j$ as the corner labelled $j \bmod 8$ is the natural identification of denominator with vertex, and it does not depend on which line is called which coordinate. Counting the corners the other way round would select a different formula.
- **The scope is formulas of this kind.** The theorems cover base 16, period 8 and simple denominators. They say nothing about π's other formulas.
- **Two rules are still open.** The two reading rules in the table above wait for a derivation.

Testing also corrected several statements in the earlier notes:
- The six-constraint derivation in [`why_pi_formal_derivation.md`](research/field-closure/why_pi_formal_derivation.md) said the geometry leaves "no freedom". In fact the order of the children is free, and only the *I Ching*'s order works.
- That derivation's "agreement rule" for the weights is really an ordered rule. No rule that depends only on whether two lines agree can produce π.
- [`IChing_Solve_Synthesis.md`](papers/09-complete-field-closure/IChing_Solve_Synthesis.md) said three geometric facts validate the trigram encoding. All three hold under every encoding. It is the ranks of the children, and Shao Yong's numbering, that fix it.

## An open invitation

Two rules remain to be derived. Why should the eldest son's line, the bottom one, count double? Why should the youngest's line, the top one, set the step around the cube? If those two rules can be read from the geometry or from the text, then the octahedron does not merely select the BBP formula: it writes it. Every other step, from the trigrams to the cube, from the cube to π's formulas, and from the TIME axis to BBP, is now on firm ground, and the tests are there for anyone who wants to try.
