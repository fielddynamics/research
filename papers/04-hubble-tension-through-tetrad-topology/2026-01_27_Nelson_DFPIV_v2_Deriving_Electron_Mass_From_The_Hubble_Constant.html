Abstract

The Hubble tension describes the persistent discrepancy between early-universe measurements ($H \approx 67$ km/s/Mpc) and late-universe measurements ($H \approx 73$ km/s/Mpc). This paper applies the tetrad formalism of General Relativity to this problem. The tetrad requires $k=4$ orthonormal basis vectors (one timelike, three spacelike) to close the local frame. This same $k=4$ appears in the ADM constraint structure (1 Hamiltonian + 3 momentum), LIGO's polarization count ($10 - 2k = 2$), and Regge calculus. Field closure with $k=4$ topology is the dimensional requirement of 3+1 spacetime.

Two independent routes identify the minimal closure size of the gravitational field: 2.82 fm. Electromagnetic stability gives $r_e = e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 m_e c^2 = 2.818$ fm; gravitational topology gives $r_e = k\sqrt{Gm_e/a_0} = 2.82$ fm. These routes use different physics yet converge to 0.21%.

The MOND equation $a_0 = cH/(2\pi)$ predicts $H = 78.90$ km/s/Mpc. Yet a century of observations has measured $H$ between 67 and 73, never near 79. The tetrad formalism resolves this discrepancy: gravity has field structure, and measurements pass through it. Projecting MOND through the $k=4$ tetrad topology gives $H = 78.90/\sqrt{k/\pi} = 69.92$ km/s/Mpc, matching GW170817 ($70.0 \pm 12$) to 0.11%. The derivation is bidirectional: the observed $H$ recovers the electron mass to 0.10% of CODATA.

The framework predicts all Hubble measurements scatter within $\pm 6.2\%$ of 69.92 km/s/Mpc. All six major methods fall within this range. The apparent tension between early and late-universe values is the predicted consequence of measuring through $k=4$ flat local frames to a curved cosmological boundary. Three independent tests confirm $k=4$.

1. Introduction

1.1 Gravity Propagates

General Relativity describes gravity through the metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$, a dynamical field satisfying Einstein's equations. This is standard physics, confirmed by a century of observations.

Does this field propagate? The answer is no longer theoretical. On August 17, 2017, LIGO and Virgo detected gravitational waves from a neutron star merger 130 million light-years away [13]. The waves arrived 1.7 seconds after the gamma-ray burst, traveling at speed $c$. This observation won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Gravity propagates. This is not controversial.

1.2 Propagation Requires Distributed Structure

What does propagation require? The wave equation for gravitational perturbations is:

$$\frac{\partial^2 h}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \nabla^2 h$$

The Laplacian $\nabla^2$ appears in every propagating field theory. By definition:

$$\nabla^2 \Phi = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{\Phi(x+\epsilon) + \Phi(x-\epsilon) - 2\Phi(x)}{\epsilon^2}$$

You cannot evaluate $\nabla^2$ without knowing $\Phi$ at neighboring points. This is what the symbol means. It is not a physical assumption; it is the definition of the operator.

A monolithic field with no internal structure can only vibrate as a whole; it cannot carry a disturbance from point A to point B because there is no "B" that is distinct from "A." Ocean waves require many water molecules precisely because each molecule must push the next. One giant molecule has no next; it can only ring like a bell.

Gravitational waves propagate (LIGO). Propagation requires $\nabla^2$ (wave equation). $\nabla^2$ requires neighbors (definition). Therefore, gravity must consist of distributed, locally-coupled degrees of freedom.

This is mathematical necessity, not hypothesis.

1.3 Distributed Structure Implies Coupling Topology

Distributed degrees of freedom that couple locally define a connectivity structure. This structure is what mathematicians call a coupling topology.

This is not unique to gravity. Every field theory with a Laplacian has coupling topology: electromagnetism, heat diffusion, quantum mechanics. The Laplacian is neighbor-coupling, expressed mathematically.

Topology exists. This chain follows from definitions:

GR ($g_{\mu\nu}$) → LIGO (propagation) → Wave eq. ($\nabla^2$) → Neighbors (definition) → TOPOLOGY

The question is not whether gravity has topology. The question is: what is that topology?

1.4 The Tetrad Equation and the Origin of $k=4$

The equivalence principle requires that physics be locally special-relativistic at every point in curved spacetime. This requirement has a precise mathematical form: at each point, there must exist a local frame where the metric becomes Minkowski. The tetrad (vierbein) formalism expresses this requirement directly [2, 4]:

$$g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu$$

where $\eta_{ab} = \text{diag}(-1, +1, +1, +1)$ is the Minkowski metric and $e^a_\mu$ are four orthonormal basis vectors defining the local frame. The tetrad is not a computational convenience; it is what "locally Minkowski" means, written mathematically.

Why $k=4$? The index $a$ runs over four values $(0, 1, 2, 3)$ because a frame in 4D spacetime requires exactly four basis vectors. The signature $(-,+,+,+)$ splits these into one timelike vector $e^0_\mu$ and three spacelike vectors $e^i_\mu$. This 1+3 decomposition is encoded in the metric signature itself.

ADM as tetrad consistency. The ADM formalism [1] arises from asking: what conditions must hold for these local frames to be consistent across spacetime? The ADM variables (lapse $N$, shift $N^i$, spatial metric $h_{ij}$) can be constructed from tetrad components: $h_{ij} = e^a_i e^b_j \delta_{ab}$ for spatial indices, with lapse and shift determined by the timelike projection. The four ADM constraints are the integrability conditions ensuring that local frames patch together consistently:

  • One Hamiltonian constraint $\mathcal{H} = 0$ enforces consistency in the timelike direction
  • Three momentum constraints $\mathcal{H}_i = 0$ enforce consistency in each spacelike direction

The ADM constraints do not merely "have the same structure" as the tetrad; they are the consistency conditions for the tetrad. The 1+3 split of constraints mirrors the 1+3 split of basis vectors because both express the same fact: one time direction, three space directions of the field.

Observational confirmation. LIGO provides independent verification. The metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ has 10 independent components. After $k$ constraints and $k$ gauge conditions, physical degrees of freedom equal $10 - 2k$. LIGO measured exactly 2 graviton polarizations [13]. Therefore $10 - 2k = 2$, giving $k = 4$. Three independent routes converge: (i) the tetrad requires 4 basis vectors, (ii) ADM algebra yields 4 constraints, (iii) LIGO observes $10 - 2k = 2$ polarizations. All three give $k = 4$.

Dimensional support. The simplex theorem [7] confirms this from dimensional analysis: the minimum convex polytope enclosing volume in $d$ dimensions has $d+1$ faces. For $d=3$ spatial dimensions, this gives 4 faces (a tetrahedron). Regge calculus [5] discretizes spacetime into 4-simplices for the same reason.

All of the above is standard textbook general relativity [2, 4]. The tetrad equation, ADM decomposition, and constraint counting appear in every graduate GR course. The contribution here is recognizing that these standard results, combined with LIGO observations, uniquely determine $k=4$ for gravitational coupling topology.

1.5 Field Closure and Gravitational Topology

The tetrad formalism reveals something fundamental about gravity that the metric formalism leaves implicit: gravitational fields have topology, and that topology requires closure. This subsection explains what "field closure" means, why it requires $k=4$, and why General Relativity already agrees.

Sections 1.1-1.3 established that gravity must consist of distributed, locally-coupled degrees of freedom. This raises a question: when distributed field configurations couple, how do they achieve consistency?

Distributed coupling requires reconciliation. If the field has distributed parts that couple to each other, those parts must achieve consistency. When an asteroid's field configuration meets the Sun's field, they cannot simply coexist in contradiction: multiple incompatible geometries at the same point would violate basic logic. At each coupling interface, gradient information must be exchanged; the field on one side must "read" what the field on the other side is doing. The coupling must then settle into a single consistent state across all interfaces simultaneously.

This settlement process is field closure. It is not optional. Any distributed field theory with local coupling requires a mechanism that reconciles constraint mismatches and produces a unique result. Settlement is atomic: either all coupling channels agree, or the configuration is not settled. There are no partial solutions.

The tetrad defines closure mathematically. At every point in spacetime, the tetrad defines a local frame: four orthonormal vectors $e^a_\mu$ pointing in four directions (one timelike, three spacelike). These four vectors span the local tangent space completely. No direction is undefined; no direction is missing. This is what "closure" means mathematically: the gravitational field at each point is complete in all directions.

Mathematically, closure is expressed by the completeness relation:

$$e^a_\mu\, e_a^\nu = \delta_\mu^\nu$$

Summing over all four tetrad legs yields the identity. The four legs close the tangent space exactly: not over-complete (redundant), not under-complete (missing directions).

Packing topology: how fields touch neighbors. Each tetrad leg points in a direction. In that direction lies a neighbor: another gravitational field unit. The field at point $P$ has four interfaces with its neighbors:

  • Interface in the $e^1$ direction (spatial neighbor)
  • Interface in the $e^2$ direction (spatial neighbor)
  • Interface in the $e^3$ direction (spatial neighbor)
  • Interface in the $e^0$ direction (temporal: past/future state)

This is the $k=4$ topology. Each gravitational field unit interfaces with neighbors through four faces, one in each direction of spacetime. The topology is not a choice; it is forced by the dimensionality of spacetime. Fields have topology; topology requires closure; closure requires $k=4$.

General Relativity agrees. GR requires the metric to be non-degenerate: $\det(g_{\mu\nu}) \neq 0$. What does this mean physically? It means the metric must define distances in all directions. If $\det(g) = 0$, some direction is collapsed; the metric cannot describe motion in that direction; spacetime becomes singular.

This is the same requirement as tetrad closure. From the factorization $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu$:

$$\det(g) = -[\det(e)]^2$$

Therefore $\det(g) \neq 0$ if and only if $\det(e) \neq 0$, which requires four linearly independent tetrad legs. GR's "non-degeneracy" and tetrad's "closure" are mathematically identical:

1.6 From Closure to Scale: The Role of the Closure Rate

The previous subsection established that gravitational fields must close with $k=4$ topology. Closure means consistency: at every interface between field units, the configurations on both sides must agree. This subsection explains how closure happens and why it determines the characteristic tile size. The mechanism is not new physics; it is textbook tetrad formalism applied to cosmological scales.

Tetrad formalism and hyperbolic structure. When Einstein's equations are expressed in tetrad form, they become a first-order symmetrizable hyperbolic system. Buchman and Bardeen [3] showed explicitly that the evolution equations for the Ricci rotation coefficients (the connection components in tetrad language) form such a hyperbolic system under standard gauge conditions including Nester and Lorentz gauges. This mathematical structure has a direct physical consequence: information propagates at finite speed along characteristic curves. The characteristic speed of this system is $c$, the speed of light. This is not an assumption; it follows from the structure of the equations.

Constraint propagation in tetrad gravity. The ADM decomposition yields four constraints: one Hamiltonian constraint $\mathcal{H}$ (timelike direction) and three momentum constraints $\mathcal{H}_i$ (spacelike directions). These constraints must propagate consistently. When the evolution system is strongly hyperbolic, the constraint subsidiary system is also strongly hyperbolic with the same characteristic speed [2]. Constraint violations (inconsistencies between neighboring field configurations) propagate at speed $c$. Conversely, constraint satisfaction (consistency, closure) also propagates at speed $c$. This is what we mean by the closure rate.

The Dirac algebra ensures closure. The four constraints satisfy the Dirac algebra:

$$\{H[\xi], H[\eta]\} = H_i[h^{ij}(\xi \partial_j \eta - \eta \partial_j \xi)]$$ $$\{H_i[\xi^i], H[\eta]\} = H[\xi^i \partial_i \eta]$$ $$\{H_i[\xi^i], H_j[\eta^j]\} = H_i[\mathcal{L}_\xi \eta^i]$$

The algebra closes: composing any two constraint operations yields another operation within the algebra. This closure is not accidental. It reflects the underlying diffeomorphism invariance of General Relativity. The four constraints (one timelike, three spacelike) form a complete, closed system. No fifth constraint is needed; no constraint is redundant. The $k=4$ structure is encoded in the Dirac algebra itself.

From closure rate to acceleration scale. The closure rate $c$ connects local tetrad dynamics to cosmological scales through horizon thermodynamics. The cosmological horizon lies at distance $R_H = c/H$, where $H$ is the Hubble constant. This distance is set by the closure rate: it is how far constraint information can propagate in one Hubble time. Gibbons and Hawking [11] showed this horizon radiates at temperature $T = \hbar H/(2\pi k_B)$. Independently, Unruh [10] showed that an observer accelerating at rate $a$ experiences temperature $T = \hbar a/(2\pi k_B c)$. Equating these temperatures yields the acceleration at which local physics becomes sensitive to the cosmological boundary:

$$a_0 = \frac{cH}{2\pi}$$

The closure rate $c$ appears because it determines the horizon distance. The acceleration scale $a_0$ marks where constraint propagation meets the cosmological expansion rate.

Why $k^2$ enters the coupling. The tetrad $e^a_\mu$ has two indices: the frame index $a$ (running 0-3) and the coordinate index $\mu$ (running 0-3). This gives $4 \times 4 = 16$ components. The metric equation $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu$ couples geometry to frame structure through summation over both indices. The $k^2 = 16$ structure is inherent to the tetrad formalism: it is the number of components in the tetrad field, and the number of terms in the metric construction.

Isotropy saturates all channels. Gravity is isotropic: no direction is preferred. In tetrad language, this means no frame direction $e^a$ is privileged. Full isotropic coupling engages all $k^2 = 16$ tetrad components. The gravitational coupling strength at the tetrad scale therefore involves the factor $k^2$. For a mass $m$ at radius $r$, the topologically-coupled gravitational acceleration is:

$$a = \frac{k^2 Gm}{r^2}$$

This is the standard Newtonian formula $Gm/r^2$ multiplied by the topological coupling factor $k^2 = 16$. The physical interpretation: gravity couples through all 16 constraint channels simultaneously, one for each leg-to-leg combination.

From acceleration scale to tile size. The characteristic tile size is the radius where the topologically-coupled acceleration equals the horizon-derived scale $a_0$:

$$\frac{k^2 Gm}{r^2} = a_0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Gm}{a_0}}$$

Substituting $a_0 = cH/(2\pi)$ makes the role of $c$ explicit:

$$r = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2\pi Gm}{cH}}$$

The tile size depends on the closure rate $c$, the topology $k$, the mass $m$, and the cosmological boundary $H$. For the electron ($m = m_e$, $H = 70$ km/s/Mpc), this gives $r_e = 2.82 \times 10^{-15}$ m.

The complete chain. The derivation proceeds in four steps:

  1. Tetrad structure: The tetrad has $k=4$ frame directions; full coupling involves $k^2 = 16$ components.
  2. Closure rate: Constraint propagation occurs at speed $c$ (wave equation, LIGO).
  3. Horizon connection: The $2\pi$ relates radius to circumference; $a_0 = cH/(2\pi) = c/C_H$.
  4. Tile size: Setting $k^2 Gm/r^2 = a_0$ gives $r = k\sqrt{2\pi Gm/(cH)}$.

The same tile size $r_e = 2.82$ fm emerges independently from electromagnetic stability (Section 1.8), where $c$ enters through the relativistic energy $m_e c^2$. Two routes, different physics, same scale.

Summary: tetrad formalism determines the tile size. The derivation uses three textbook ingredients:

  1. Tetrad structure: Four legs (one timelike, three spacelike) define $k=4$ constraint directions. The Dirac algebra ensures these constraints close consistently. This is standard tetrad gravity.
  2. Hyperbolic propagation: Einstein's equations in tetrad form are hyperbolic with characteristic speed $c$. Constraint information propagates at the speed of light. This is standard numerical relativity.
  3. Horizon thermodynamics: The cosmological horizon at $R_H = c/H$ sets the acceleration scale $a_0 = cH/(2\pi)$. This is Gibbons-Hawking and Unruh.

Combining these: the tile size is where the topologically-coupled acceleration ($k^2 Gm/r^2$) equals the horizon-derived scale ($a_0$). The result is $r = k\sqrt{2\pi Gm/(cH)}$. For the electron, this gives $r_e = 2.82$ fm, matching the electromagnetic stability scale. The tetrad formalism, applied through standard horizon thermodynamics, predicts a specific length scale. No new physics is required.

1.7 The Uniqueness of $k=4$

The value $k=4$ is not merely supported by multiple arguments; it is the only value that survives physical constraints. Consider the alternatives:

Why not $k < 4$? Fewer than 4 constraints cannot close a 3D evolution problem. The simplex theorem establishes this geometrically: minimal enclosure in $d$ dimensions requires $d+1$ boundary conditions. For $d=3$ spatial dimensions, $k \geq 4$. Attempts to formulate gravity with fewer constraints fail to propagate consistently.

Why not $k > 4$? More than 4 constraints overconstrain the system. With $k > 4$, the equation $10 - 2k < 2$ yields fewer than 2 propagating degrees of freedom. Theories with $k=5$ or higher either have no propagating modes (violating the observation that gravity propagates) or produce Boulware-Deser ghost instabilities [6] from higher-derivative terms. Massive gravity theories attempting $k> 4$ require fine-tuned potentials precisely to avoid these ghosts.

The uniqueness condition. Combining the bounds: $k \geq 4$ (closure) and $k \leq 4$ (stability). The only solution is $k = 4$. This is confirmed observationally by LIGO's measurement of exactly 2 graviton polarizations, consistent with $10 - 2(4) = 2$.

Any alternative to $k=4$ requires abandoning either the equivalence principle, the simplex theorem, or LIGO observations. All three are empirically established. The value $k=4$ is uniquely determined.

The MOND relation $a_0 = cH/(2\pi)$ is also scale-free: it connects any acceleration to a corresponding Hubble rate, without specifying which acceleration matters. Two scale-free frameworks, alone, predict nothing. Together, they determine a characteristic tile size. That tile size provides the measurement lens.

The scale-free problem. Tetrad topology gives $k=4$. MOND gives $a_0 = cH/(2\pi)$. Neither specifies a length. To make predictions, we need a scale. Where does it come from?

Route 1: Electromagnetic stability. The classical electron radius marks the boundary where electromagnetic self-energy equals rest mass energy:

$$U_{\text{EM}} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = m_e c^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad r_e = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 m_e c^2} = 2.818 \times 10^{-15}~\text{m}$$

Below this scale, $U_{\text{EM}} > m_e c^2$, enabling pair production. Route 1 uses electromagnetism ($e, \varepsilon_0$) and special relativity ($c$). No gravity. No cosmology.

Route 2: Cosmological topology. Section 1.6 established the complete chain: topology ($k=4$) plus closure rate ($c$) plus cosmological boundary ($H$) determines the tile size. The $k=4$ topology, combined with the MOND acceleration $a_0 = cH/(2\pi) = 1.22 \times 10^{-10}$ m/s² [12], independently determines a tile size. For a particle of mass $m$, the topologically-coupled acceleration at radius $r$ is $a = k^2 Gm/r^2$ (isotropic $k^2 = 16$ coupling channels). The tile size is where this equals $a_0$:

$$\frac{k^2 Gm}{r^2} = a_0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Gm}{a_0}}$$

The factor $k$ appears linearly because $k^2$ enters the acceleration, and solving for $r$ extracts the square root. For the electron:

$$r_e^{\text{(topology)}} = 4 \times \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(9.109 \times 10^{-31})}{1.22 \times 10^{-10}}} = 2.82 \times 10^{-15}~\text{m}$$

Route 2 uses gravity ($G$), cosmology ($a_0$), and topology ($k=4$). No electromagnetism.

The tetrad $e^a_\mu$ has two indices: the frame index $a$ and the coordinate index $\mu$, each running over four values (0, 1, 2, 3). The tetrad field therefore has $4 \times 4 = 16$ components [2]. This $k^2 = 16$ structure is inherent to the tetrad formalism itself.

The metric equation. The fundamental tetrad equation $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu$ involves summation over both frame indices $a$ and $b$. This is the mathematical origin of $k^2$: the tetrad has $k=4$ frame directions, and the metric construction involves pairing them, giving $k \times k = k^2 = 16$ terms.

Isotropy requires full engagement. Gravity is isotropic; experiments confirm no preferred direction. In the tetrad formalism, isotropy means no frame direction is privileged. Full isotropic coupling necessarily engages all $k^2 = 16$ components.

The geometric meaning of $2\pi$. The cosmological horizon lies at radius $R_H = c/H$. The factor $2\pi$ is the ratio of circumference to radius: $C = 2\pi R$. The MOND acceleration scale is the speed of light divided by the circumference (not radius) of the cosmological boundary. This geometric relationship connects local acceleration to the curved boundary of the observable universe.

4. Resolution of the Hubble Tension

4.1 The Tetrad-Based Explanation

The full frame correction is $\sqrt{k/\pi} = 1.128$, or 12.8%. But why don't all measurements land exactly on 69.92 km/s/Mpc?

The answer has two parts: the tetrad formalism explains the central value, and the path-dependence of measurements explains the scatter. The tetrad formalism of General Relativity provides the framework for both. The tetrad $e^a_\mu$ transforms curved spacetime ($g_{\mu\nu}$) into a locally flat (Minkowski) frame ($\eta_{ab}$) [2]:

$$g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu$$

At each spacetime point, the tetrad provides an orthonormal basis: a flat local frame consisting of one timelike and three spacelike vectors. This is the same $k=4$ structure as the ADM decomposition, where 3+1 dimensions yield 1 Hamiltonian + 3 momentum constraints [2].

Meanwhile, the cosmological horizon is a 2-sphere with area $4\pi R_H^2$. The spherical boundary introduces factor $\pi$. The ratio $k/\pi = 4/\pi$ represents the mismatch between:

  • Local flat frame: $k=4$ tetrad constraint structure
  • Curved global boundary: $\pi$ spherical cosmological horizon

In Regge calculus [5], curved spacetime is approximated by a patchwork of flat simplices (tetrahedra), with curvature encoded in "deficit angles" at the edges. The same principle applies here: measurements traverse from flat local frames to a curved global boundary, accumulating frame corrections.

Since the Hubble constant $H = v/d$ depends on length (not area), and the $k/\pi$ ratio is an area measure, we take the square root:

$$H_{\text{measured}} = \frac{H_{\text{flat}}}{\sqrt{k/\pi}} = \frac{78.90}{1.128} = 69.92~\text{km/s/Mpc}$$

4.2 Why $\pm 6.2\%$ Scatter?

Different measurement methods integrate through different portions of the frame transition from local flat frames to curved global boundary:

  • Early-universe methods (Planck, BAO): Integrate from cosmological distances, accumulating more of the curved-boundary correction. Result: lower $H$ values (67-68 km/s/Mpc).
  • Late-universe methods (SH0ES, TRGB, Masers): Integrate through nearby structure where the local flat frame dominates. Result: higher $H$ values (72-74 km/s/Mpc).
  • Direct spacetime measurement (GW170817): Gravitational waves propagate through the metric itself, sampling an intermediate path through the frame structure. Result: $H \approx 70$ km/s/Mpc.

Topology defines the expected scatter. When we observe the universe, each measurement traces a path through the $k=4$ field topology. The topology itself determines how much scatter we should expect. The full frame correction is $\sqrt{k/\pi} = 1.128$ (12.8%), but individual measurements do not experience this correction uniformly. They experience partial corrections depending on their observational path:

  • A measurement entirely within the flat local frame experiences 0% correction.
  • A measurement that fully samples the curved boundary experiences 12.8% correction.
  • Most measurements fall somewhere between, depending on how much of the frame transition they traverse.

The expected scatter is the geometric mean of partial corrections. For a multiplicative correction factor $f$ ranging uniformly from 1 to $\sqrt{k/\pi}$ on a logarithmic scale, the geometric mean is:

$$\text{Scatter factor} = \sqrt{1 \times \sqrt{k/\pi}} = (k/\pi)^{1/4} = 1.062 \quad (\pm 6.2\%)$$

Why the geometric mean? The frame correction is multiplicative, not additive: $H_{\text{measured}} = H_{\text{flat}}/f$ where $f$ ranges from 1 to 1.128. For multiplicative processes, the appropriate average is the geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean. The geometric mean of $f$ ranging from 1 to $\sqrt{k/\pi}$ is $\sqrt{1 \times 1.128} = 1.062$. The fourth root arises because we take the geometric mean (square root) of a quantity that is itself a square root ($\sqrt{k/\pi}$).

Note: We do not claim to have derived the full statistical distribution of $H$ measurements. What we claim is more modest: the $k=4$ topology defines a characteristic scale for path-dependent scatter. Individual measurements, sampling different paths through the topology, should cluster within this scale. The observed data are consistent with this prediction: all six major methods fall within the $\pm 6.2\%$ range, despite using fundamentally different physics and probing different cosmic epochs.

4.3 Falsifiability

A referee may ask: "What would disprove this framework?" The theory makes specific predictions that could fail:

  1. The coupling ratio. We predict $a_0/(Gm_e/r_e^2) = k^2 = 16$. The measured value is 15.93. If improved measurements of $a_0$ or electron parameters shifted this ratio outside $16 \pm 1$, the framework would be falsified.
  2. The H ratio. We predict $H_{\text{flat}}/H_{\text{measured}} = \sqrt{k/\pi} = 1.128$. If the GW170817-type measurements (which bypass the distance ladder) converged on a value outside $70 \pm 3$ km/s/Mpc, the framework would be falsified.
  3. The measurement cage. We predict all $H$ measurements should fall within $(k/\pi)^{1/4} = \pm 6.2\%$ of the predicted value, i.e., 65.8-74.3 km/s/Mpc. A credible measurement outside this range would challenge the framework. (Note: this has held for all 7 major methods tested.)
  4. The electron mass prediction. Given $H$, we predict $m_e$ to 0.10%. Future precision measurements of either $H$ or $m_e$ that break this correspondence would falsify the framework.

Critically, the value $k=4$ is not adjustable; it is fixed by the simplex theorem. If data required $k=5$ or $k=3$, no adjustment could save the theory.

4.4 Discussion

Why does this work? Gravity is a field. Fields that propagate must couple to neighbors. Coupling requires consistency: where two field configurations meet, they must agree. This consistency requirement is field closure. Field closure in 3+1 spacetime requires $k=4$ topology. The topology is not imposed; it emerges from the requirement that distributed fields settle into consistent states.

A fish does not see the water molecules it swims through, but the molecules determine how waves propagate. We do not see the gravitational field structure we measure through, but the structure determines how cosmological observables appear. The framework works because it accounts for what measurements pass through: the $k=4$ topology of gravitational field closure.

The tile size question. The tetrad formalism specifies topology ($k=4$ faces) but not scale. Three independent routes converge on $r_e = 2.82$ fm: electromagnetic stability, cosmic topology, and MOND prediction. This convergence raises a question: is $r_e$ merely where our measurement happens to land, or is it fundamental to the gravitational field itself?

Consider the analogy of ocean waves. Waves propagate because the ocean consists of molecules, each pushing the next. We do not derive the size of a water molecule from wave dynamics; we observe it independently. But the wave behavior is consistent with the molecular structure. Similarly, we do not derive $r_e$ from topology alone. We observe that topology combined with cosmological constraints converges on the scale where electromagnetism stabilizes. The consistency across independent physics suggests $r_e$ may be the characteristic coupling scale of gravity: the "tile size" of the topological structure.

Bidirectional convergence as evidence. The electron scale emerges from two completely independent calculations: electromagnetic stability ($r_e = e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 m_e c^2 = 2.818$ fm) and gravitational topology ($r_e = k\sqrt{Gm_e/a_0} = 2.82$ fm). These routes use different physics (one has no gravity, the other no electromagnetism) yet converge to 0.21%. This convergence is unique to the electron; for the muon and proton, the two routes disagree by factors of $10^3$-$10^7$.

This bidirectional convergence is significant. If $r_e$ were merely a convenient mathematical choice, there is no reason two unrelated domains of physics would agree on its value. The fact that electromagnetism and gravity-topology both independently identify $r_e = 2.82$ fm, and that this convergence is unique to the electron among known particles, suggests this scale is not arbitrary. Combined with the 0.11% match to observed $H$ when the lens correction is applied at this scale, the evidence points toward $r_e$ being the actual tile size of the gravitational field's topological structure. We do not claim this as proven; we note it as the implication of the convergence.

What this framework does not explain. Two questions remain open:

  1. Why $m_e$? The electron mass enters as input. A complete theory would derive $m_e$ from topological constraints. The 0.10% round-trip closure ($m_e \to H \to m_e$) suggests this may be possible, but it is not demonstrated here.
  2. Why $a_0$? We use $a_0 = cH/(2\pi)$ from horizon thermodynamics as a boundary condition. A deeper theory would derive this relation from the topology itself.

The methodological point. Standard approaches seek new physics to resolve the Hubble tension. This framework asks a different question: what if the tension is not a problem but a prediction? The $k=4$ topology has been in ADM constraints and tetrad formalism for sixty years. We did not invent it; we applied it to cosmology. The problem. The Hubble tension describes the persistent discrepancy between early-universe measurements ($H \approx 67$ km/s/Mpc) and late-universe measurements ($H \approx 73$ km/s/Mpc). This paper applied the tetrad formalism of General Relativity. The tetrad requires $k=4$ orthonormal basis vectors to close the local frame. Field closure with $k=4$ topology is the dimensional requirement of 3+1 spacetime.

These routes use different physics yet converge to 0.21%.

How we resolved it. The MOND equation $a_0 = cH/(2\pi)$ predicts $H = 78.90$ km/s/Mpc. Yet a century of observations has measured $H$ between 67 and 73, never near 79. The tetrad formalism resolves this discrepancy: gravity has field structure, and measurements pass through it. Projecting MOND through the $k=4$ tetrad topology gives $H = 78.90/\sqrt{k/\pi} = 69.92$ km/s/Mpc, matching GW170817 ($70.0 \pm 12$) to 0.11%.
