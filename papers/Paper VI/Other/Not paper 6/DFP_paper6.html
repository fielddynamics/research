1.1 What General Relativity Already Tells Us

We begin with established physics. General Relativity, formulated by Einstein in 1915, describes gravity through the metric tensor \(g_{\mu\nu}\), a field defined at every point in spacetime. The tetrad formalism, which describes the local structure of this field through four orthonormal basis vectors, appears in every graduate GR textbook: Misner, Thorne, Wheeler (1973), Wald (1984), Carroll (2004). The ADM decomposition, which separates spacetime into 3 spatial dimensions + 1 temporal dimension with exactly 4 constraint equations, has been standard since 1962.

From these foundations, a key property follows: gravity is a distributed field. This is not a new proposal; it is what the mathematics requires. The metric tensor \(g_{\mu\nu}(x)\) is defined at every spacetime point \(x\). The Einstein field equations are partial differential equations, relating field values at one point to field values at neighboring points. A field governed by differential equations must have values at different locations that can be compared.

The Logic of Distribution

Consider what General Relativity asserts:

  1. Gravity is a field, described by the metric tensor \(g_{\mu\nu}\)
  2. A field must be either monolithic or distributed: one value everywhere, or values at each point
  3. GR's equations are differential, relating values at neighboring points
  4. Therefore, gravity is distributed. This is what the mathematics says.

If gravity were monolithic (a single value with no internal structure), it could not satisfy differential equations. There would be no "neighboring points" to compare. The field would have no gradients, no curvature, no dynamics. GR's mathematical structure requires distribution.

1.2 What LIGO Gave Us: A Way to See

On September 14, 2015, LIGO's mirrors trembled. Gravitational waves from two merging black holes, 1.3 billion light-years away, had arrived as ripples in spacetime. This detection confirmed what GR had predicted: gravitational disturbances propagate at the speed of light.

LIGO did not prove that gravity is distributed; the mathematics already required that. What LIGO gave us was something different: a visualization. We can now see gravity's distributed nature in action, the way we see ocean waves reveal the ocean's molecular structure. This visualization will guide us through the paper.

1.3 The Ocean Analogy

To understand what "distributed structure" means for gravity, consider a familiar example: the ocean.

It is a vast network of water molecules, each linked to its neighbors through hydrogen bonds.

Each water molecule reaches out to four neighbors in a tetrahedral arrangement: two hydrogen atoms donating bonds, two sites on the oxygen accepting them. In ice, this tetrahedral network is rigid and crystalline. In liquid water, it is dynamic: bonds constantly forming, breaking, reforming. The structure fluctuates, yet the four-fold coordination persists.

This is what LIGO showed us about gravity. When gravitational waves traveled 1.3 billion light-years to reach Earth, they were not the universe "ringing." This analogy will recur throughout the paper. When we measure the Hubble constant, we are measuring through gravitational field structure, like measuring ocean properties by observing surface waves. The structure we measure through affects what we observe.

2.1 The Wave Equation

How do disturbances travel through a field? Whether we are describing light, sound, heat, or gravity, the answer takes the same mathematical form: the wave equation. For gravitational perturbations \(h\) in the weak-field limit:

\[\frac{\partial^2 h}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \nabla^2 h\]

The left side, \(\partial^2 h/\partial t^2\), describes how the field accelerates in time, how quickly its rate of change is changing. The right side, \(c^2 \nabla^2 h\), describes how the field curves in space, how the value at a point differs from its surroundings. The constant \(c\) sets the propagation speed.

This equation says: temporal acceleration equals spatial curvature times speed squared. When the field is curved in space (higher in some places, lower in others), that curvature drives temporal change. The disturbance propagates.

LIGO confirmed this equation experimentally. GW170817, the neutron star merger observed on August 17, 2017, produced gravitational waves that arrived 1.7 seconds after the gamma-ray burst from the same event, verifying that gravity propagates at speed \(c\) to extraordinary precision.

2.2 What the Laplacian Means

The wave equation contains the Laplacian operator \(\nabla^2\). This operator appears throughout physics: in electromagnetism, heat conduction, quantum mechanics, and General Relativity. To understand why topology is required, we must understand what this operator means.

The Laplacian measures how a field value at a point differs from the average of its surroundings. In one dimension, the definition is:

\[\nabla^2 \Phi = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{\Phi(x+\epsilon) + \Phi(x-\epsilon) - 2\Phi(x)}{\epsilon^2}\]

Look at the numerator: \(\Phi(x+\epsilon) + \Phi(x-\epsilon) - 2\Phi(x)\). This compares the value at \(x\) to values at two neighboring points, \(x+\epsilon\) and \(x-\epsilon\). The expression measures: "Is the value at \(x\) higher or lower than the average of its neighbors?"

WHAT THE LAPLACIAN PROCESSES ══════════════════════════ Φ(x-ε) Φ(x) Φ(x+ε) ● ● ● │ │ │ ────────┼──────────────────┼──────────────────┼──────── x-ε x x+ε ∇²Φ = [Φ(x+ε) + Φ(x-ε) - 2Φ(x)] / ε² Translation: "How does the value at x compare to its neighbors?" • If Φ(x) equals the neighbor average → ∇²Φ = 0 (flat) • If Φ(x) is below the neighbor average → ∇²Φ > 0 (valley) • If Φ(x) is above the neighbor average → ∇²Φ < 0 (peak)

In three dimensions, you need six neighbors (±x, ±y, ±z). The principle is the same: the Laplacian compares a point to its surroundings.

The Key Requirement

To evaluate \(\nabla^2\Phi\) at any point, you must know \(\Phi\) at neighboring points. This is not a physical assumption; it is what the symbol means. A field with no neighbors has no Laplacian. A field with no Laplacian cannot satisfy the wave equation. A field that cannot satisfy the wave equation cannot propagate.

A structure with distributed degrees of freedom that couple locally is called a coupling topology. This is the mathematical term for "things with parts that talk to neighbors."

3.1 Route One: Geometry (The Simplex Theorem)

We begin with pure geometry. What is the minimum structure needed to enclose volume in three dimensions?

The answer comes from combinatorial geometry. In \(d\) dimensions, the minimum convex polytope that can enclose volume has \(d+1\) vertices and \(d+1\) faces. This shape is called the d-simplex:

THE SIMPLEX IN EACH DIMENSION ════════════════════════════ DIMENSION SIMPLEX NAME VERTICES FACES CAN ENCLOSE? ───────── ──────────── ──────── ───── ──────────── 1D Line segment 2 2 No (1D has no "volume") 2D Triangle 3 3 No (flat, no interior) 3D Tetrahedron 4 4 YES (first closed 3D shape) Formula: For d dimensions, minimum enclosure requires d+1 faces For 3D space: k_min = 3 + 1 = 4

This is a mathematical theorem, not a physical hypothesis. Consider trying to enclose volume with only 3 faces: you get a triangle, which is flat. It has edges and vertices but no interior. The tetrahedron, with 4 faces, is the first shape that has an "inside."

The Grocery Store Demonstration

You can see this theorem every time you pass a fruit display. Place three oranges on a table so they touch. They naturally arrange into a triangle. Now place a fourth orange on top. It settles into the pocket formed by the three below, touching all three simultaneously.

This is sphere packing, the geometry that grocers use intuitively and crystallographers study formally. When identical spheres pack as closely as possible, they form tetrahedral local arrangements. The centers of any four mutually-touching spheres define a tetrahedron.

The same structure appears throughout nature. Carbon atoms in diamond bond to four neighbors in tetrahedral coordination. Silicon in semiconductors does the same. Methane (CH₄) is tetrahedral. Water molecules form tetrahedral hydrogen-bond networks. The number 4 is not chosen; it is the minimum that geometry permits for 3D closure.

Why Not More Than 4?

Higher coordination numbers are possible: cubes have 6 faces, octahedra have 8. But these require additional symmetry constraints. The tetrahedron is the minimum, the simplest structure that achieves 3D closure. When nature optimizes for simplicity, it chooses the minimum.

3.2 Route Two: General Relativity (The ADM Constraints)

The second route comes from the structure of General Relativity itself. How many independent constraints does GR impose on the gravitational field?

The ADM formalism (Arnowitt, Deser, Misner 1962) answers this question by decomposing spacetime into space and time. This "3+1 decomposition" treats the three spatial dimensions and one time dimension separately, yielding exactly four constraint equations:

  • 1 Hamiltonian constraint \(\mathcal{H} = 0\): governs how the field evolves in time
  • 3 Momentum constraints \(\mathcal{H}_i = 0\): govern how the field is arranged in space

What "3+1" Means

The "3+1" in "3+1 spacetime" refers to 3 dimensions of space plus 1 dimension of time. GR treats these differently: space is where things exist; time is how things evolve. The ADM formalism makes this split explicit. One constraint (Hamiltonian) governs time evolution; three constraints (momentum) govern spatial arrangement. The 1+3 structure of the constraints mirrors the 1+3 structure of spacetime itself.

Why exactly 4 constraints, and not some other number? The answer lies in representation theory. Under spatial rotations:

  • The Hamiltonian constraint transforms as a scalar (1 component)
  • The momentum constraints transform as a vector (3 components)

Scalars and vectors are irreducible representations. They cannot mix because they transform differently under rotations. You cannot combine one scalar equation and three vector equations into fewer constraints. The 1+3 = 4 structure is locked in by the mathematics of symmetry.

3.3 Route Three: LIGO (Degree of Freedom Counting)

The third route is empirical. How many physical degrees of freedom does gravity have? LIGO answered this question.

The metric tensor \(g_{\mu\nu}\) is a symmetric 4×4 matrix, giving 10 independent components. But not all of these are physical. Some are removed by constraints (the ADM equations), and some are gauge artifacts (coordinate choices). The counting is:

\[\text{Physical DOF} = 10 - 2k\]

where \(k\) is the number of constraints (which equals the number of gauge freedoms in GR). To find \(k\), we need to know how many physical degrees of freedom gravity has.

LIGO provided the answer: gravitational waves have exactly 2 polarizations, the "plus" (+) and "cross" (×) modes. This is not approximately 2; it is precisely 2, as GR predicts for a massless spin-2 field.

Setting physical DOF equal to 2:

\[10 - 2k = 2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad k = 4\]

LIGO's measurement of exactly 2 graviton polarizations empirically fixes \(k=4\).

4.1 The Tetrahedron: Simplest 3D Enclosure

The tetrahedron is the simplest three-dimensional solid. It has:

  • 4 vertices: the corner points
  • 4 faces: each an equilateral triangle
  • 6 edges: connecting the vertices

A key property: all four faces are geometrically equivalent. The structure has no preferred direction. This isotropy matches a fundamental property of gravity. In empty space, gravity does not prefer one direction over another. The tetrahedral structure naturally encodes this symmetry.

4.2 Mapping Faces to Constraints

Section 3.2 showed that GR has exactly 4 constraint equations: 1 Hamiltonian constraint (governing time evolution) and 3 momentum constraints (governing spatial arrangement). Each of these constraints corresponds to one face of the tetrahedron.

The connection comes through the tetrad formalism, a standard technique in GR that describes spacetime through four orthonormal basis vectors at each point: one timelike vector (\(e^0\)) pointing in the time direction, and three spacelike vectors (\(e^1, e^2, e^3\)) pointing in spatial directions.

The tetrad equation relates these basis vectors to the metric:

\[g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu\]

Here \(\eta_{ab} = \text{diag}(-1, +1, +1, +1)\) is the flat Minkowski metric. This equation says: at any point, curved spacetime locally looks like flat spacetime when viewed through the tetrad frame. The four tetrad vectors are the four "directions" that define local flatness.

In the geometric picture: when all four faces of the tetrahedron are "settled," the local field state is consistent. The tetrahedron is not merely an analogy for \(k=4\); it is the geometric structure that \(k=4\) describes.
