3.1 Road One: Stability (The Parity Argument)

Why would a single tetrahedron fail as a field structure? The answer lies in symmetry: a tetrahedron has a preferred direction, and this preference creates instability. To see why, we must examine how field flux distributes across the faces.

A tetrahedron has 4 faces. In the context of spacetime structure, one face differs from the others: the face opposite the apex. We designate this the TIME face and the remaining three as SPATIAL faces. (This naming is motivated in Section 4.5 and justified in Section 5.4; for now, the key point is that one face is structurally distinguished.)

Each face carries a field intensity (flux). Let \(\Phi_{\text{TIME}}\) and \(\Phi_{\text{SPATIAL}}\) denote these fluxes, with \(\delta\phi = \Phi_{\text{TIME}} - \Phi_{\text{SPATIAL}}\) as their difference. Let \(\hat{n}_{\text{TIME}}\) be the outward normal vector of the TIME face. The stability calculation follows directly:

The Stability Calculation

For a single tetrahedron with face fluxes:

  • Net flux vector: \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum_i \Phi_i \times \hat{n}_i\)
  • For a tetrahedron, face normals sum to zero: \(\sum \hat{n}_i = 0\)
  • Therefore: \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = (\Phi_{\text{TIME}} - \Phi_{\text{SPATIAL}}) \times \hat{n}_{\text{TIME}} = \delta\phi \times \hat{n}_{\text{TIME}}\)
  • Since \(\delta\phi \neq 0\): \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}} \neq 0\) (NOT EQUILIBRIUM)

The solution is to add a second tetrahedron with opposite orientation. The second tetrahedron has its TIME face pointing in the opposite direction: \(\hat{n}_{\text{TIME}}^B = -\hat{n}_{\text{TIME}}^A\).

Single tetrahedron (UNSTABLE):

\[\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \delta\phi \times \hat{n}_{\text{TIME}} \neq 0\]

Two tetrahedra (STABLE):

\[\vec{F}_{\text{total}} = \delta\phi \cdot \hat{n}_A + \delta\phi \cdot \hat{n}_B = \delta\phi \cdot (\hat{n}_A + \hat{n}_B) = 0\]

(Since \(\hat{n}_B = -\hat{n}_A\), we have \(\hat{n}_A + \hat{n}_B = 0\))

Equilibrium is restored. The two opposite orientations cancel, eliminating the ghost and stabilizing the structure. Road 1 establishes that stability requires two tetrahedra with opposite orientations.

"A necessary and sufficient condition for a non-compact spacetime to admit a spinor structure is that it possesses a global field of orthonormal tetrads." Geroch (1968), J. Math. Phys., 611 citations [10]

The theorem has three key components:

The connection to two tetrahedra follows from the double-cover structure. For every rotation \(g \in \text{SO}(1,3)\), there exist two distinct elements \(\pm s \in \text{SL}(2,C)\). These differ by a sign. They are genuinely different, not the same element counted twice.

Structural translation: Define a "closure" as one complete field cycle through a tetrahedron. Then:

3.6 The Compatibility Condition

\[g^{\mu\nu} E^A_\mu L^B_\nu = g^{\mu\nu} E^B_\mu L^A_\nu\]

The symbols \(E\) and \(L\) represent the tetrad (basis vectors) of each metric. The condition says that when you combine them using metric \(g\), the result must be symmetric. In plain language: the two tetrahedra must be "mirror compatible" at their interface.

The symmetric vielbein condition is equivalent to requiring that the matrix \(\sqrt{g^{-1}f}\) be real. Topologically, this means there must exist a well-defined "middle ground" between the two metrics, a region where they can consistently interact.
