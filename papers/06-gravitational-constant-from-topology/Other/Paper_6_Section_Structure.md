### 2.1 Road One: Stability (The Parity Argument)

**Textbook source:** Alexander, Marcianò, & Smolin (2014); Speziale (2010)

**The argument:**
- A tetrahedron has 4 faces: 3 SPATIAL + 1 TIME (the 3+1 structure)
- *Note:* We define TIME and SPATIAL faces in Section 3.5 and justify these names in Section 4.4. For now, the key point is that one face (opposite the apex) differs from the other three
- Each face carries a field intensity (flux). The TIME face carries a different intensity than SPATIAL faces
- **The stability calculation:**
  - Let Φ_TIME and Φ_SPATIAL be the fluxes, with δφ = Φ_TIME - Φ_SPATIAL (the difference)
  - Let n_TIME be the outward normal vector of the TIME face
  - Net flux through single tetrahedron: F_net = δφ × n_TIME
  - Since δφ ≠ 0 (TIME differs from SPATIAL): F_net ≠ 0
  - **This is NOT equilibrium—the structure is unstable**
- **Textbook confirmation:** "The parity symmetric phase yields... a scalar ghost. Because of the latter this phase is unstable." (Alexander et al. 2014)
- **Solution:** Add second tetrahedron with opposite orientation
  - The second tetrahedron has its TIME face pointing opposite: n_TIME_B = -n_TIME_A
  - F_A + F_B = δφ × n_A + δφ × (-n_A) = 0
  - **Equilibrium restored**

**Key equation:**
```
Single tetrahedron: F_net = δφ × n_TIME ≠ 0  (UNSTABLE)
Two tetrahedra:     F_total = F_A + F_B = 0   (STABLE)
```

### 2.2 Road Two: The Double Cover (Spinor Requirement)

**Textbook sources:** Weinberg (1995); Penrose & Rindler (1984); Geroch (1968)

**Background definitions:**
- *SO(1,3):* The Lorentz group—all rotations and velocity boosts in 4D spacetime
- *SL(2,C):* The group of 2×2 complex matrices with determinant 1
- *Double cover:* SL(2,C) "covers" SO(1,3) twice—for every Lorentz transformation, there are TWO corresponding SL(2,C) elements (±s)

**The argument:**
- Established physics: Spinors (like electrons) transform under SL(2,C), not SO(1,3)
- **Physical consequence:** A spinor requires 720° rotation (not 360°) to return to its original state
- For every rotation g ∈ SO(1,3), there exist TWO elements ±s ∈ SL(2,C)
- These two elements are DISTINCT—they differ by a sign

**Structural translation:**
- A "closure" is one complete field cycle through a tetrahedron
- One tetrahedron traversed twice gives IDENTICAL closures—the same field configuration repeated
- Two DIFFERENT tetrahedra give two DISTINCT closures—genuinely different field configurations
- The double cover requires two distinct structures, not one structure traversed twice

**Key insight:** "A necessary and sufficient condition for a spacetime to admit a spinor structure is that it possesses a global field of orthonormal tetrads." (Geroch 1968)

### 2.3 Road Three: Chirality (Ashtekar Decomposition)

**Textbook sources:** Ashtekar (1986); Barbero (1995); Jacobson & Smolin (1987)

**Background definitions:**
- *Chirality:* Handedness. A chiral object differs from its mirror image (like left and right hands)
- *Self-dual (A⁺):* Associated with right-handed rotations
- *Anti-self-dual (A⁻):* Associated with left-handed rotations
- *Weyl spinors:* The simplest spinor objects; come in left-handed and right-handed varieties
- *⊕ (direct sum):* The full object is built from both parts together

**The argument:**
- The gravitational connection decomposes: A = A⁺ ⊕ A⁻ (self-dual + anti-self-dual)
- Left-handed Weyl spinors couple to A⁻; right-handed couple to A⁺
- Full gravity requires BOTH chiral halves
- **Key insight from Ashtekar:** "The left-handed spin connection is sufficient to code gravitational degrees of freedom" (Jacobson & Smolin 1987)
- But FULL dynamics requires both halves—you cannot describe complete gravity with only one chirality

**Structural translation:**
- Tetrahedron A (one orientation) ↔ one chirality (A⁺)
- Tetrahedron B (opposite orientation) ↔ opposite chirality (A⁻)
- The stellated octahedron naturally realizes both chiralities

**Connection to parity:**

- *Why parity?* This is not arbitrary. When two regular tetrahedra share all 8 cube vertices, they naturally partition into odd-parity and even-parity sets.
