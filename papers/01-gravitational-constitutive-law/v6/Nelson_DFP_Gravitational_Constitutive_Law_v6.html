1. Introduction

When you measure the gravitational pull of a galaxy, something unexpected happens: the gravity is stronger than the visible matter can explain. This discrepancy appears everywhere we look, from individual galaxies to the largest structures in the universe. The standard explanation invokes a new substance, "dark matter," that interacts gravitationally but remains invisible. An alternative explanation, MOND, modifies how gravity works at very low accelerations. Both approaches have had successes and failures. What neither has done is explain why the mathematical function that governs the transition takes the form it does.

This paper derives that function from physical principles. The result, \(\mu(x) = x/(1+x)\), emerges as the unique solution within a well-defined class of gravitational coupling mechanisms. The derivation requires no curve-fitting, no free parameters, and no appeal to dark matter.

Third, we preview how field closure constraints uniquely determine the constitutive law (§1.3).

1.1 The Observational Puzzles

The mass discrepancy in galactic dynamics is one of the central problems in astrophysics. Across five decades of galaxy mass and morphological type, observations reveal a consistent pattern: gravitational effects exceed what visible matter can produce. This discrepancy manifests in multiple, independent ways:

Rotation curves remain flat. In galaxy after galaxy, stars at the outer edge orbit just as fast as stars much closer to the center. Newtonian mechanics predicts velocities should decline with distance from the center, much like planets in our solar system, where distant planets orbit more slowly than inner ones. Galactic rotation curves do not decline. They remain flat, or even rise, far beyond the visible disk.

Gravitational lensing exceeds visible mass. Light bending around galaxies and clusters implies gravitational pulls 5–10 times stronger than the visible matter can produce. The inferred "extra" mass is not optional; it is required by the geometry of spacetime itself.

Scaling relations are remarkably tight. The Baryonic Tully–Fisher Relation (BTFR) connects a galaxy's total baryonic mass to its asymptotic rotation velocity: \(M_b \propto V^4\). This relation holds across five decades in mass with remarkably little scatter. If the "missing mass" were an independent substance distributed arbitrarily, why would it correlate so precisely with visible matter?

Cluster dynamics require something extra. Galaxy clusters move as if they contain far more mass than observed in stars and hot gas. The temperature of intracluster gas implies deep gravitational wells, deeper than visible matter can explain.

These observations demand explanation. The dominant hypothesis, particle dark matter, posits a new substance that interacts gravitationally but not electromagnetically. Yet an alternative phenomenology exists: Milgrom's Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), which modifies the gravitational response below a characteristic acceleration scale \(a_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10}\) m/s². MOND successfully predicts the tight BTFR, the shape of rotation curves, and the radial acceleration relation across a wide range of galaxy types, without invoking new particles.

2.0 Why Field Closure?

Before examining the architecture, we establish why field closure is necessary at all. The argument is simple but foundational.

Gravity is a field. General Relativity describes gravity through the metric tensor gμν, a dynamical field satisfying Einstein's equations. This is not controversial.

Fields that propagate must be distributed. LIGO detected gravitational waves arriving from a neutron star merger 130 million light-years away. Waves traveled from there to here; the field disturbance at the source affected detectors on Earth. This requires the field to have spatially distributed structure: a monolithic field with no internal parts could only vibrate everywhere at once, not carry a signal from point A to point B.

Distributed coupling requires reconciliation. If the field has distributed parts that couple to each other, those parts must achieve consistency. When the asteroid's field configuration meets the Sun's field, they cannot simply coexist in contradiction. Multiple incompatible geometries at the same point would violate basic logic. At each coupling interface, gradient information must be exchanged: the field on one side must "read" what the field on the other side is doing. The coupling must then settle into a single consistent state across all interfaces simultaneously.

This settlement process is what we call field closure. It is not optional. Any distributed field theory with local coupling requires a mechanism that reconciles constraint mismatches and produces a unique result. Settlement is atomic: either all coupling channels agree, or the configuration is not settled. There are no partial solutions. We examine this mechanism in detail because understanding HOW settlement must work, what constraints it satisfies, determines the constitutive law μ(x).

Mathematically, this distributed structure appears in the Poisson equation \(\nabla^2 \Phi = 4\pi G\rho\). The Laplacian operator \(\nabla^2\) is defined by neighbor comparison:

\[ \nabla^2 \Phi = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{\Phi(x+\epsilon) + \Phi(x-\epsilon) - 2\Phi(x)}{\epsilon^2} \]

You cannot evaluate \(\nabla^2 \Phi\) without knowing \(\Phi\) at neighboring points. This is what the symbol means. Every appearance of \(\nabla^2\) in gravitational physics carries with it an implicit requirement: the field must have spatially distributed values that can be compared. Field closure names the process by which these distributed parts reach agreement.

2.0.1 The Constraint Structure of General Relativity

Section 2.0 established that gravity must be distributed. This section asks: how distributed? General Relativity gives a precise answer. The mathematics of GR severely limits what constitutive laws are viable, and motivates the postulates we use in Section 3.

The Wave Equation Argument

LIGO detected gravitational waves. Waves propagate. Think about what propagation actually requires: a disturbance at one location must influence an adjacent location, which influences the next, and so on. A wave is fundamentally a chain of neighbors talking to neighbors.

The mathematics makes this precise. Any wave equation contains the Laplacian operator:

\[ \nabla^2 \Phi = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{\Phi(x+\epsilon) + \Phi(x-\epsilon) - 2\Phi(x)}{\epsilon^2} \]

Look at what this formula asks for: the field value to the left, the field value to the right, and the field value here. You cannot compute \(\nabla^2\Phi\) at a point without knowing \(\Phi\) at neighboring points. The Laplacian is a comparison operator. It measures how a point differs from its surroundings.

This means a propagating field cannot be monolithic. A single global value could ring at one frequency, but it could not carry a wave from here to there. Wave propagation requires the field to have parts: distinct degrees of freedom at different locations, coupled to their neighbors.

This is not a postulate. It follows directly from LIGO's observations and the definition of propagation.

The Simplex Theorem: Why k = 4

If gravity consists of distributed parts that must achieve consistency, how many connections does each part need? Think of it this way: to fence off a region, how many posts do you need at minimum?

On a line, two posts bracket an interval. On a plane, three posts form a triangle enclosing area. In space, four posts form a tetrahedron enclosing volume. You cannot do better. Try to enclose a 3D region with three points and you get a flat triangle with no interior volume.

Theorem (Simplex): In \(d\) dimensions, the minimum number of points required to enclose a region is \(d + 1\).

For three-dimensional space: \(k = d + 1 = 4\). This is Euclidean geometry, known since antiquity. The tetrahedron is the simplest solid. Any system that must achieve local consistency in 3D space needs at least four coupling channels to "close" around a region.

The ADM Formalism: k = 4 in General Relativity

The number four appears again when we look at how General Relativity actually works. The ADM formalism slices 4D spacetime into 3D snapshots evolving in time, like frames of a film. Einstein's equations then split into two types: evolution equations that say how each frame changes into the next, and constraint equations that each frame must satisfy internally.

How many constraints? Exactly four:

  • One energy constraint (the Hamiltonian \(\mathcal{H} = 0\)): energy must balance
  • Three momentum constraints (\(\mathcal{H}_i = 0\)): momentum must balance in x, y, z

Why 1 + 3? Energy is a scalar: it has no direction. Momentum is a vector: it points somewhere in 3D space. You cannot combine them because they transform differently under rotations. The split is forced by the geometry of space itself.

This same four appears everywhere in gravitational physics. The tetrad formalism uses four basis vectors (one timelike, three spacelike) at each point. Regge calculus discretizes spacetime into 4-simplices. Numerical relativity codes mesh space with tetrahedra. These are not independent coincidences. They all reflect the same underlying fact: satisfying constraints in 3+1 dimensional spacetime requires four channels.

From Constraint Structure to Postulates

The \(k = 4\) structure does not uniquely determine the postulates in Section 3, but it strongly constrains them and motivates their form.

Postulate A1 says gravity reduces to Newton at high accelerations. This is not topology; it is experiment. We measure Newtonian gravity in laboratories and the solar system. Any theory must recover this.

Postulate A2 says coupling capacity saturates at some scale \(a_0\). Think of it this way: if each point can only talk to four neighbors, there is a limit to how much information it can process per unit time. Finite connectivity implies finite throughput. The existence of a saturation scale follows from \(k = 4\); the specific value \(a_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10}\) m/s² requires additional physics (cosmological horizon or microphysics).

Postulate A3 says saturation occurs one channel at a time. This is parsimony, not derivation. With four independent constraints, you could imagine models where multiple channels saturate together, but such models add parameters without necessity. We start with the simplest case. If observations require more complexity, we add it.

Field closure names the process by which these four constraints achieve simultaneous satisfaction. The ADM constraints ARE field closure, expressed in GR's canonical language. Our question is: what constitutive law governs the closure response when the system is pushed toward its capacity limit?

Appendix A: Formal Lemmas (Proof Spine)

This appendix formalizes the proof sketches in §3 into short lemmas. Throughout, let \(x \equiv |\nabla\Phi|/a_0\) denote the dimensionless acceleration ratio, and let \(\mu(x)\) be the constitutive factor in the AQUAL equation.

Assumptions (Minimal Single-Channel Closure)

The following lemmas assume the MSC class defined in §3.1:

  • MSC1: Single occupancy variable \(\varepsilon \in [0,1]\)
  • MSC2: Forward rate \(\propto x^n\) and available capacity \((1-\varepsilon)\)
  • MSC3: Reverse rate \(\propto \varepsilon\) (single-channel relaxation)
  • MSC4: Constitutive identification \(\mu(x) \equiv \varepsilon_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)\)

Scope: Theorem A.5 proves uniqueness within MSC, not uniqueness among all possible closure mechanisms.

Lemma A.1 (Detailed-Balance Equilibrium)

Assume: A single occupancy variable \(\varepsilon \in [0,1]\) with reversible transitions between "unresolved" \((1-\varepsilon)\) and "resolved" \((\varepsilon)\) states, with rates

\[r_+(x,\varepsilon) = \alpha x^n (1-\varepsilon), \quad r_-(\varepsilon) = \beta \varepsilon\]

for some \(\alpha, \beta > 0\) and \(n > 0\).

Then: The unique equilibrium satisfies

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eq}}(x) = \frac{x^n}{k + x^n}, \quad k \equiv \beta/\alpha.\]

Proof. Detailed balance requires \(r_+ = r_-\), giving \(\alpha x^n (1-\varepsilon) = \beta \varepsilon\). Solving for \(\varepsilon\) yields \(\varepsilon = \alpha x^n / (\alpha x^n + \beta) = x^n/(x^n + k)\). Uniqueness follows because the balance equation is linear in \(\varepsilon\) with \(\alpha, \beta > 0\). ∎

Lemma A.2 (Deep-MOND Scaling Forces \(n = 1\))

Assume: The constitutive identification \(\mu(x) \equiv \varepsilon_{\mathrm{eq}}(x)\), and the empirical deep-MOND boundary condition \(\mu(x) \sim x\) as \(x \to 0\) (Postulate 2).

Then: \(n = 1\), and \(k\) is fixed by the choice of \(a_0\).

Proof. From Lemma A.1, as \(x \to 0\): \(\mu(x) = x^n/(k + x^n) \approx x^n/k\). The deep-MOND condition \(\mu(x) \sim x\) forces \(n = 1\). The remaining factor \(1/k\) fixes the normalization of \(x\), equivalently a rescaling of \(a_0\), so one may set \(k = 1\) by definition of the acceleration scale. ∎

Lemma A.3 (Unique \(\mu\) in MSC)

Under the hypotheses of Lemmas A.1–A.2, \(\mu(x)\) is uniquely determined:

\[\mu(x) = \frac{x}{1+x}.\]

Proof. Lemma A.1 gives \(\mu(x) = x^n/(k + x^n)\). Lemma A.2 forces \(n = 1\) and \(k = 1\) (by normalization). Substitute to obtain \(\mu(x) = x/(1+x)\). ∎

Lemma A.4 (Newtonian Correspondence)

The \(\mu(x)\) in Lemma A.3 satisfies the Newtonian limit (Postulate 1): \(\mu(x) \to 1\) as \(x \to \infty\).

Proof. From \(\mu(x) = x/(1+x)\): as \(x \to \infty\), \(\mu \to x/x = 1\). ∎

Theorem A.5 (Constitutive Law in MSC)

In the Minimal Single-Channel Saturating Closure (MSC) class defined in §3.1, the interpolating function is uniquely determined as

\[\mu(x) = \frac{x}{1+x}.\]

Proof. Lemma A.1 establishes the equilibrium form under detailed balance. Lemma A.2 fixes \(n = 1\) via the deep-MOND boundary condition. Lemma A.3 yields the explicit \(\mu\). Lemma A.4 verifies the Newtonian limit. The result follows. ∎
