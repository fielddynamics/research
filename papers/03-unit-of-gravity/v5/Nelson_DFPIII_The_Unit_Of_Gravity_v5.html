1. Introduction

Papers I and II established that gravity operates through distributed field closure with \(k=4\) tetrahedral topology. But topology alone cannot determine a physical scale. This paper asks: what sets the tile SIZE? The answer connects gravitational topology to the electron scale through a remarkable convergence of independent physics.

1.1 The Question

Gravity has topology. The number \(k=4\) appears throughout gravitational physics:

  • Simplex theorem: The minimum 3D enclosure has \(d+1 = 4\) faces
  • ADM formalism: 1 Hamiltonian + 3 momentum constraints
  • Tetrad formalism: 1 timelike + 3 spacelike basis vectors
  • LIGO polarizations: \(10 - 2k = 2\) independent modes

Papers I and II derived this topology from first principles. But \(k=4\) is a pure number. It tells us the gravitational tile is tetrahedral. It does not tell us how big that tetrahedron is. A tetrahedron can be atomic-scale or cosmological-scale; the topology is identical.

The Central Question

Topology gives \(k=4\). What gives the tile SIZE?

This paper answers that question. Two independent routes, using completely different physics, converge on the same scale: \(r_e = 2.82\) fm.

Why understanding the mechanism matters. Consider an analogy. A GPU returns correct computational results. Knowing this is sufficient for using the GPU. But understanding HOW it achieves those results reveals architecture: thousands of local threads processing in parallel, each handling its piece independently. This architectural insight enables optimization, extension, and prediction in ways that "it works" cannot.

The same principle applies here. Knowing THAT the topology is \(k=4\) suffices for many calculations. But understanding HOW this topology determines a specific scale reveals deeper structure: the crossover between gravitational topology and electromagnetic stability at the electron scale. This is what connects the tile size to testable predictions about the Hubble constant.

2. From Shape to Size

Section 1 established the question: topology gives \(k=4\), but what gives the tile SIZE? This section builds the bridge. We have two ingredients, each powerful but incomplete. Connecting them requires understanding how gravity couples through the tetrad structure, which leads directly to the crossover condition that Section 3 will use.

2.1 Two Ingredients, Neither Complete

We have two pieces of physics that should determine the gravitational tile. Neither, on its own, can give us a length.

Ingredient 1: Topology gives a pure number. The simplex theorem states that the minimum convex polytope enclosing volume in \(d\) dimensions has \(d+1\) faces. For 3D space: \(k = d + 1 = 4\). This is the tetrahedron, the simplest possible closed volume.

The number \(k=4\) is dimensionless. It appears at every scale, from molecular (CH₄, SiO₄) to cosmological (Regge calculus tiles spacetime with 4-simplices). It tells us the gravitational tile is tetrahedral. It cannot tell us how big that tetrahedron is.

Ingredient 2: MOND gives an acceleration, not a length. Paper II derived \(a_0 = cH/(2\pi)\) from horizon thermodynamics: the Unruh temperature at acceleration \(a_0\) equals the Gibbons-Hawking temperature of the cosmological horizon. This gives a characteristic acceleration:

\[a_0 = \frac{cH}{2\pi} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m/s}^2\]

But acceleration has units of m/s², not meters. The MOND scale tells us where the crossover between Newtonian and non-Newtonian regimes occurs. It does not specify which particle or which radius sets the tile size.

The gap. We have shape (\(k=4\)) and we have an acceleration scale (\(a_0\)). To get a length, we need to connect them. The connection comes from understanding how gravity actually couples through the \(k=4\) topology.

2.2 How Gravity Couples: The Tetrad Structure

The \(k=4\) topology is not merely a counting exercise. It has physical consequences. To see this, we use the tetrad formalism, which is standard graduate-level general relativity (Misner, Thorne & Wheeler; Wald).

Why tetrads? The equivalence principle requires physics to be locally special-relativistic at every point in curved spacetime. The tetrad (or vierbein) formalism makes this explicit. At each point, we define four orthonormal basis vectors \(e^a_\mu\) that span the local tangent space. The metric is then:

\[g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu\]

Here \(\eta_{ab} = \text{diag}(-1, +1, +1, +1)\) is the flat Minkowski metric. The tetrad converts between the curved spacetime (indices \(\mu, \nu\)) and the local flat frame (indices \(a, b\)). This is the same physics as standard GR, expressed in different language:

Concept GR Language Tetrad Language
Field defined everywhere Non-degenerate metric Closure
Mathematical condition \(\det(g) \neq 0\) \(\det(e) \neq 0\)
Structure 3+1 decomposition 4 tetrad legs
Constraints 4 ADM equations 4 projections onto legs
Packing topology (not named) \(k=4\) faces
Table 1: GR and tetrad formalisms express the same physics. Both require fields to be complete in four directions.

Where does \(k^2 = 16\) come from? The tetrad \(e^a_\mu\) has two indices. The frame index \(a\) runs from 0 to 3 (four values). The coordinate index \(\mu\) also runs from 0 to 3 (four values). This gives \(4 \times 4 = 16\) independent components.

When the metric equation couples geometry to frame structure, it sums over both indices. The \(k^2 = 16\) is not a fit parameter; it is the number of components in the tetrad field, which follows from the dimensionality of spacetime.

Isotropy engages all channels. Gravity is isotropic: no spatial direction is preferred. In tetrad language, this means no frame direction \(e^a\) is privileged. Full isotropic coupling therefore engages all \(k^2 = 16\) tetrad components simultaneously.

For a mass \(m\) at radius \(r\), the topologically-coupled gravitational acceleration is the Newtonian value multiplied by the coupling factor:

\[a_{\text{coupled}} = k^2 \cdot \frac{Gm}{r^2} = 16 \cdot \frac{Gm}{r^2}\]

This is the key result: the \(k=4\) topology amplifies the gravitational coupling by a factor of \(k^2 = 16\). Gravity couples through all 16 constraint channels, one for each combination of tetrad legs.

2.3 The Crossover Condition

Now we can connect the two ingredients. Where does the topologically-coupled gravitational acceleration equal the MOND scale? This crossover defines the characteristic tile size.

Setting the coupled acceleration equal to \(a_0\):

\[k^2 \cdot \frac{Gm}{r^2} = a_0\]

Solving for \(r\):

\[r^2 = \frac{k^2 \, Gm}{a_0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Gm}{a_0}}\]

The Crossover Condition

\[r = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Gm}{a_0}}\]

This defines a characteristic length scale for any mass \(m\). The factor \(k\) appears because \(k^2\) enters the acceleration, and taking the square root extracts one power of \(k\).

Why this works. The crossover condition takes the two scale-free ingredients and combines them in the only way that produces a length:

  • Topology contributes \(k = 4\) (dimensionless)
  • MOND contributes \(a_0\) (units: m/s²)
  • Gravity contributes \(G\) and \(m\) (units: m³/kg·s² and kg)
  • The combination \(\sqrt{Gm/a_0}\) has units of meters

Why the electron? The crossover condition defines a length for any mass \(m\). Which mass matters? The electron is the smallest stable particle that satisfies the crossover. It is a probe, not a cause, like a thermometer that reveals temperature but does not create it. We measure through the electron because it inhabits the scale where topology meets cosmology.

3. Two Routes to the Tile Size

Section 2 established the crossover condition: \(r = k\sqrt{Gm/a_0}\). This section applies it. We derive the tile size from two completely independent domains of physics that should have nothing to do with each other. The fact that they converge to 0.21% is the central result of this paper.

3.1 Route 1: Electromagnetic Stability

The first route comes from pure electromagnetism. It asks: at what scale does the electron's electromagnetic field energy equal its rest mass? This question involves no gravity, no cosmology, no topology.

The physical picture. A charged particle creates an electric field that extends outward. This field contains energy. The closer you get to the charge, the stronger the field, and the more energy is stored. At some radius, the electromagnetic energy stored in the field equals the particle's rest mass energy.

Electromagnetic self-energy of a charged sphere of radius \(r\):

\[U_{\text{EM}} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\]

Rest mass energy of the electron:

\[E = m_e c^2\]

Setting \(U_{\text{EM}} = E\) and solving for \(r\):

Route 1 Result:

\[r_e = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 m_e c^2} = 2.818 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m} = 2.818 \text{ fm}\]

Constants used: \(e, \varepsilon_0, m_e, c\)
Domain: Electromagnetism + special relativity
No gravity. No cosmology. No topology.

This is the classical electron radius, a textbook result dating to Lorentz. It defines the scale where electromagnetic self-interaction becomes significant.

3.2 Route 2: Gravitational Topology

The second route comes from the crossover condition derived in §2.3. It asks: at what scale does topologically-coupled gravitational acceleration equal the MOND scale? This question involves no electromagnetism.

The crossover condition (from §2.3):

\[r = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Gm}{a_0}}\]

Applying to the electron with measured values:

  • \(k = 4\) (tetrahedral closure, from simplex theorem)
  • \(m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\) kg (electron mass)
  • \(a_0 = 1.22 \times 10^{-10}\) m/s² (MOND empirical scale)
  • \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\) m³/(kg·s²) (gravitational constant)

Calculation:

\[r_e = 4 \times \sqrt{\frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(9.109 \times 10^{-31})}{1.22 \times 10^{-10}}}\]

Route 2 Result:

\[r_e = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{Gm_e}{a_0}} = 2.82 \times 10^{-15} \text{ m} = 2.82 \text{ fm}\]

Constants used: \(G, k, m_e, a_0\)
Domain: Gravity + cosmology + topology
No electromagnetism.

3.3 The Convergence

Compare the two results. Route 1 uses electromagnetism and special relativity. Route 2 uses gravity, cosmology, and topology. They share only one constant: the electron mass \(m_e\). Yet they agree to four significant figures:

Route Constants Used Result Domain
1 (EM) \(e, \varepsilon_0, m_e, c\) 2.818 fm Electromagnetism
2 (Grav) \(G, k, m_e, a_0\) 2.82 fm Gravity + Cosmology
Convergence 0.21%
Table 2: Two routes to the tile size. Different physics, same scale.

Why this convergence is remarkable. The two routes use different physics. They share no constants except \(m_e\). Route 1 knows nothing about gravity or the cosmological horizon. Route 2 knows nothing about electric charge or the permittivity of free space. Yet they land on the same scale.

Why this convergence is unique. The agreement is specific to the electron. For the muon (\(m_\mu = 207 m_e\)), the two routes disagree by a factor of ~10³. For the proton (\(m_p = 1836 m_e\)), they disagree by ~10⁷. Only for the electron do electromagnetic stability and gravitational topology meet at the same scale.

The Central Result

Two independent physics domains converge on \(r_e = 2.82\) fm to 0.21%. This is the unit of gravity: the tile size of the gravitational field.

What the convergence enables. Because the two routes agree, we can derive \(a_0\) from first principles. The derivation chain runs from pure topology to the MOND acceleration scale:

What this proves. The coefficient 16 is not a fit parameter. It is \(k^2 = (d+1)^2 = 4^2\), where \(d = 3\) is the dimension of space. The only empirical inputs are measured values of \(G\), \(m_e\), and \(r_e\). The formula itself follows from the tetrad structure.

3.4 Paper IV Confirmation

Paper IV provides a third independent route using the tetrad formalism. When Einstein's equations are expressed in tetrad form, they become a hyperbolic system with characteristic speed \(c\). Field constraints propagate at the speed of light; we call this the "closure rate."

The cosmological horizon lies at distance \(R_H = c/H\), set by how far constraint information can propagate in one Hubble time. Combining the closure rate \(c\) with the cosmological boundary \(H\) gives:

\[r_e = k \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2\pi Gm_e}{cH}} = 2.82 \text{ fm}\]

Three routes. Three different combinations of physics. Same result. The tile size is not assumed; it is derived.

4. The Topological Connection

The tile size derivation reveals a deeper topological structure: the ratio \(\sqrt{k/\pi}\) that connects flat-space MOND to cosmological observables. This section explores why two \(a_0\) derivations differ by this ratio, what it means physically, and how Paper IV applied it to predict \(H_{\text{measured}}\).

4.1 The Two a₀ Values

There are two independent routes to the MOND acceleration scale:

Cosmological route (horizon thermodynamics):

\[a_0^{\text{cosmo}} = \frac{cH}{2\pi} = 1.08 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m/s}^2\]

Topological route (tile size derivation):

\[a_0^{\text{geom}} = k^2 \cdot \frac{Gm_e}{r_e^2} = 1.22 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m/s}^2\]

These differ by 12%. But look at the ratio:

\[\frac{a_0^{\text{geom}}}{a_0^{\text{cosmo}}} = \frac{1.22}{1.08} = 1.130\]

4.2 The √(k/π) Ratio

The observed ratio matches a simple topological expression:

\[\sqrt{\frac{k}{\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi}} = \sqrt{1.273} = 1.128\]

Match: 0.1%

This is not coincidence. The ratio has physical meaning:

  • \(k = 4\): Tetrahedral closure (flat, discrete, local)
  • \(\pi\): Spherical horizon (curved, continuous, global)
  • \(\sqrt{k/\pi}\): Frame correction between these geometries

Why the square root? The Hubble constant \(H = v/d\) has units of inverse time, which depends on length. The ratio \(k/\pi\) compares topological characteristics (area-like quantities). Since area scales as length squared, we take the square root to convert to a length correction.

The √(k/π) Ratio

\(\sqrt{k/\pi} = 1.128\) is the frame correction between flat local frames (characterized by \(k=4\) tetrahedral closure) and the curved cosmological boundary (characterized by \(\pi\) from the spherical horizon).

MOND operates in flat space—it was derived from galactic dynamics, where spacetime curvature is negligible. Cosmological observations, however, project through curved spacetime to reach the spherical horizon. The ratio connects these two regimes.

4.3 Testable Prediction: The Hubble Constant

The \(\sqrt{k/\pi}\) ratio connects the tile size derivation to a testable cosmological prediction. From the MOND equation \(a_0 = cH/(2\pi)\), we can predict the Hubble constant:

Paper III Prediction

According to the MOND equation:

\[H = \frac{2\pi a_0}{c} = \frac{2\pi \times (1.22 \times 10^{-10})}{3 \times 10^8} = 78.90 \text{ km/s/Mpc}\]

This is a testable prediction. The MOND acceleration scale \(a_0\), derived from galactic dynamics, predicts a specific value for the Hubble constant.

The observational puzzle: A century of observations measures \(H \approx 70\) km/s/Mpc, not 79. This 12% discrepancy requires explanation.

The resolution: The \(\sqrt{k/\pi} = 1.128\) ratio—identified in this paper from the convergence of the two \(a_0\) derivations—provides the explanation. This is documented in the Historical Note below.
