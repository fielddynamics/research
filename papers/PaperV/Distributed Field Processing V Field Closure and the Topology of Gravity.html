In Regge calculus, discrete spacetime is built from simplices with, again, four vertices in three dimensions.

The number four is not arbitrary. It appears because we live in 3+1 dimensional spacetime. But textbooks typically present these appearances independently, as separate mathematical facts in separate formalisms. The deeper question remains: what unifies them?

2. Textbook Foundations

Before introducing any new interpretation, we must establish what mainstream physics already knows. This section cites only textbook sources. Everything here can be found in standard graduate courses on general relativity and quantum gravity.

2.1 The ADM Constraint Structure

The Arnowitt-Deser-Misner (ADM) formalism recasts Einstein's equations into Hamiltonian form by decomposing spacetime into spatial slices evolving in time. This "3+1 decomposition" introduces variables: the spatial metric \(\gamma_{ij}\), the lapse function \(N\), and the shift vector \(N^i\). The extrinsic curvature \(K_{ij}\) describes how each slice bends in the embedding spacetime.

Einstein's equations become constraint equations plus evolution equations. The constraints are:

Hamiltonian Constraint:

\[\mathcal{H} = \sqrt{\gamma}\left(R^{(3)} + K^2 - K_{ij}K^{ij}\right) - 16\pi G \sqrt{\gamma}\rho = 0\]

Momentum Constraints:

\[\mathcal{H}_i = -2\nabla_j\left(\sqrt{\gamma}(K^j_i - \delta^j_i K)\right) + 16\pi G \sqrt{\gamma}j_i = 0\]

The Hamiltonian constraint enforces consistency in the timelike direction. The three momentum constraints enforce consistency in each spatial direction. Together, these four equations must hold on every spatial slice for the geometry to represent a valid solution to Einstein's equations.

The key point: The 1+3 structure is not optional. It reflects the 1+3 structure of spacetime itself, one time dimension plus three space dimensions.

2.2 The Tetrad Formalism

The equivalence principle requires that physics be locally special-relativistic at every point. This has a precise mathematical expression: at each spacetime point, there must exist an orthonormal frame where the metric becomes Minkowski. The tetrad (or vierbein) formalism makes this explicit.

A tetrad consists of four vector fields \(e^a_\mu\), where \(a = 0, 1, 2, 3\) labels the frame direction and \(\mu\) labels spacetime coordinates. The metric is reconstructed from the tetrad:

\[g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{ab}\, e^a_\mu\, e^b_\nu\]

Here \(\eta_{ab} = \text{diag}(-1, +1, +1, +1)\) is the flat Minkowski metric. The tetrad "squares" to give the curved metric. The four vectors decompose as one timelike (\(e^0\)) and three spacelike (\(e^1, e^2, e^3\)), mirroring the 1+3 structure of the ADM formalism.

The tetrad is complete: summing over all four legs gives the identity:

\[e^a_\mu\, e_a^\nu = \delta_\mu^\nu\]

No fifth leg is needed; no leg is redundant. The tangent space at each point is exactly spanned by four orthonormal vectors.

2.3 Loop Quantum Gravity: The Quantum Tetrahedron

Loop Quantum Gravity represents quantum states of geometry as spin networks: graphs with edges labeled by spins \(j\) and nodes labeled by intertwiners. The remarkable result, established by Rovelli, Vidotto, and others, is that these abstract mathematical objects have direct geometric interpretation.

The Quantum Tetrahedron

"Each 4-valent node of a spin network corresponds to a quantum tetrahedron." Rovelli & Vidotto, 2014

A 4-valent node has four edges meeting at a point. Each edge carries angular momentum quantum numbers. The node represents a quantum of volume, and its four edges represent the four faces of a tetrahedron. The intertwiner at the node encodes how these faces fit together consistently.

The closure constraint in LQG states that the total angular momentum at each node must vanish:

\[\hat{J}_1 + \hat{J}_2 + \hat{J}_3 + \hat{J}_4 = 0\]

This quantum constraint has a classical counterpart. If we assign area \(A_f\) and outward normal \(\hat{n}_f\) to each face of a tetrahedron, Minkowski's theorem (1897) states:

\[\sum_{f=1}^{4} A_f \hat{n}_f = 0\]

This is not merely an analogy. The closure constraint is the quantum version of a classical geometric identity: the area-weighted normals of any closed polyhedron sum to zero.

2.4 Regge Calculus: Discrete Gravity

Tullio Regge showed in 1961 that general relativity can be formulated on simplicial complexes without coordinates. In Regge calculus, spacetime is approximated by flat simplices glued together, with curvature concentrated at the hinges (edges where simplices meet).

In three spatial dimensions, the building block is the tetrahedron. In four-dimensional spacetime, the building block is the 4-simplex (pentachoron), which is bounded by five tetrahedra. The curvature at each hinge is measured by the deficit angle \(\epsilon_h\), and the Regge action is:

\[S = \sum_h A_h \epsilon_h\]

Regge calculus is not a new theory; it is a discrete approximation to standard GR that becomes exact in the continuum limit. Its significance for our purposes is that it demonstrates the tetrahedron is the natural building block for discrete 3D geometry.

2.6 The Simplex Theorem: Why k=4 Is Geometrically Necessary

The answer lies in classical geometry. The simplex theorem establishes a fundamental fact about enclosure in any dimension:

The Simplex Theorem

In \(d\) dimensions, \(d+1\) points are required to enclose a bounded region. Fewer points cannot form a closed boundary; more points are redundant.

The progression is intuitive. Consider building up dimension by dimension:

1D (line) 2 Interval Length
2D (plane) 3 Triangle Area
3D (space) 4 Tetrahedron Volume
4D (spacetime) 5 Pentachoron 4-volume
Table 3: Dimensional progression of the simplex.

The pyramid, or tetrahedron, is the simplest three-dimensional shape that encloses volume. Two points give a line segment, which has no interior. Three points give a triangle, which bounds area but has no thickness. Four points, provided they are not coplanar, give a tetrahedron, which bounds a three-dimensional interior.

This is not a choice or convention. It is mathematical necessity. You cannot enclose 3D volume with fewer than four non-coplanar points. The tetrahedron is the minimal solid.

This explains k=4. The ADM constraints are four because spacetime has 3+1 dimensions. The tetrad has four legs because four vectors span 4D tangent space. LQG tetrahedra have four faces because four is the minimum for 3D enclosure. Regge simplices have four vertices for the same reason. The number four is not coincidence; it is geometric necessity inherited from the dimensionality of spacetime.

3. From Constraint to Mechanism

Section 2 established what textbooks tell us: closure exists, it requires \(k=4\), and this follows from dimensional necessity. Now we ask a different question: what does closure do? If we reinterpret constraints as active processes rather than passive equations, what picture emerges?

3.1 Constraints as Coupling Channels

Consider the four ADM constraints not as equations to be satisfied but as interfaces through which information flows. The Hamiltonian constraint connects a spatial slice to its temporal neighbors. The three momentum constraints connect each point to its spatial neighbors in three independent directions.

In this reinterpretation, "constraint must be satisfied" becomes "coupling must complete." The constraint equation describes the condition that must hold when coupling is finished. But a condition implies a process: something happens to bring about that condition.

The Reinterpretation

Textbook view: Constraints are equations that the geometry must satisfy.
Mechanism view: Constraints are coupling channels through which field configurations achieve consistency.

This is not changing the mathematics. The same equations hold. We are changing the interpretation: from static conditions to dynamic processes.

3.4 The Two Physical Requirements: Lossless and Local

The settlement process is constrained by observational facts about gravity:

Lossless: Gravitational coupling produces no drag, no wake, no energy dissipation. A planet orbiting a star does not slow down from gravitational friction. The MICROSCOPE experiment verified the equivalence principle to \(10^{-15}\) precision, confirming that gravitational coupling is lossless. Local: Causality prohibits faster-than-light information transfer. The settlement at boundary \(\Sigma\) cannot depend on distant regions that are outside the light cone. All information needed for closure must be present at \(\Sigma\) itself. This is not a design choice; it is forced by special relativity.

Together, these requirements constrain the mechanism: closure must be complete (lossless) and self-contained (local). The four coupling channels at \(\Sigma\) must carry all the information needed for settlement.

5. The Port Ratio: Sphere to Tetrahedron

The Field Envelope is characterized by continuous, spherical geometry with signature \(\pi\). The Field Chamber is characterized by discrete, tetrahedral geometry with signature \(k=4\). These are different geometric languages. The Port Ratio translates between them.

5.1 Continuous Geometry: The Field Envelope (\(\pi\))

The Field Envelope is naturally spherical because gravity is isotropic: no direction is preferred. The sphere is the unique shape with this symmetry. All formulas involving spherical geometry contain \(\pi\):

  • Circumference: \(2\pi r\)
  • Surface area: \(4\pi r^2\)
  • Volume: \(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\)

The number \(\pi\) is the signature of continuous, curved geometry. It appears whenever we measure around or across a curved boundary.

5.2 Discrete Topology: The Coupling Ports (\(k=4\))

The Field Chamber has four faces, the minimum for 3D enclosure. Unlike the sphere, the tetrahedron is discrete: it has a specific number of vertices (4), edges (6), and faces (4). Its geometry is characterized by the integer \(k=4\), not by \(\pi\).

This discreteness is fundamental. LQG shows that area and volume are quantized at the Planck scale. Regge calculus approximates smooth spacetime with discrete simplices. The tetrahedron represents the irreducible quantum of 3D geometry.

5.3 The Port Ratio: \(\sqrt{k/\pi}\)

How do we convert between these geometric languages? The ratio of their signatures provides the conversion factor:

\[\text{Port Ratio} = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi}} = 1.128\]

The square root appears because the quantities we convert (like length, which determines the Hubble constant \(H = v/d\)) depend on distance, not area. Area scales as length squared, so length scales as the square root of area. To convert quantities that depend on length, we take the square root of the geometric ratio.

Definition: Port Ratio

The Port Ratio \(\sqrt{k/\pi} = 1.128\) is the conversion factor between continuous (Field Envelope) and discrete (Field Chamber) geometry. It relates measurements made through spherical boundaries to measurements made through tetrahedral interfaces.

The Port Ratio has a concrete geometric meaning. If you take a spherical Field Envelope and ask "what is the working volume accessible through \(k=4\) Coupling Ports?", the answer involves the Port Ratio.

The Port Ratio captures a similar relationship: the ratio between what continuous geometry predicts and what discrete topology permits.

The practical application appears in cosmology. The MOND relation \(a_0 = cH/(2\pi)\) uses spherical geometry (the \(2\pi\) relates horizon circumference to radius). But actual coupling goes through discrete ports. The mismatch is exactly \(\sqrt{k/\pi}\):

\[H_{\text{measured}} = \frac{H_{\text{continuous}}}{\sqrt{k/\pi}} = \frac{78.90}{1.128} = 69.92 \text{ km/s/Mpc}\]

The mathematical closure of this round-trip provides strong evidence that the chain is not coincidental but reflects underlying physical structure.

The measured ratio is 15.93; the prediction is 16. Match: 0.41%.
